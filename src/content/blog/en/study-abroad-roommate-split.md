Moving into a flatshare abroad makes money management a step harder than it was. Rent and utilities are in the local currency, money from home arrives in another, and you probably still have small debts outstanding with friends back home. Three streams running at once.

The mistake most people make is converting everything into their home currency and keeping one combined ledger. It's an understandable instinct, but **the moment you manage local living costs in your home currency, you stop being able to talk to your flatmates about them**. A record you can't show the other people in the flat isn't much use in a shared household.

## Keep the record in the local currency

Anything shared with flatmates gets recorded in the currency it was paid in. If rent is charged locally, the record is local. Converting is something you do afterwards for your own purposes, not something you do before settling up.

The reason is simple: **the moment you convert, your numbers stop matching theirs**. Whatever rate you used, the figure you're looking at and the figure your flatmate is looking at will differ. Once a shared record contains that drift, every check turns into a conversion exercise.

This is the same principle as splitting costs on a trip, covered in [splitting bills in different currencies](/en/blog/split-bills-different-currencies/). Studying abroad differs only in that it runs for a year or more rather than a week.

## Convert a few times a year, not monthly

So when do you convert? Only when you want to check your own budget. Monthly isn't necessary.

While you're abroad, the exchange rate moves under your feet. The same amount arriving from home buys a different amount locally from month to month. Track the converted figure every month and you'll lose the ability to tell whether you spent more or the rate simply moved, which generates anxiety about something you didn't do.

The number to watch is the local one. **Whether you spent more in local currency than last month is the only part that responds to your behaviour.** The rate doesn't, so checking it once a quarter is plenty. Choosing which rate to use is covered in [which exchange rate to use when splitting](/en/blog/exchange-rate-for-splitting/).

## Settle with flatmates entirely on the local side

Keep flatshare settlement inside the local banking system and local payment apps. Routing it through your home account costs you both days and fees.

Even if you can't open a local bank account, getting whatever payment app people actually use locally is worth doing early. Matching what your flatmates already use produces the least friction. Asking them to adopt a payment method from your country isn't realistic, so you're the one who adapts.

For dividing the rent itself, if the rooms differ in size, the area-ratio approach in [splitting rent by room size](/en/blog/roomshare-rent-split/) applies. Student flatshares often have very uneven rooms, so it's worth settling this in the first week.

## Don't mix in your home-country account

The classic way a study-abroad budget falls apart is treating home spending and local spending as one pot. Your phone plan back home, subscriptions, a storage unit for the things you left behind. All of it keeps going out while you're away.

Handle those as a separate category. Keep them out of anything shared with flatmates and manage them as your own costs. Mixed together, amounts irrelevant to the flat end up inside the shared record, and every review requires filtering them back out.

The same applies to money from home. That's income, not expenditure, so it doesn't belong in the shared record at all.

## A month in a three-person flatshare

Rent of €2,100, €180 for utilities and internet, and €300 of groceries bought together, split three ways. The rooms are comparable, so it's an even split.

| Item | Total | Per person |
|---|---|---|
| Rent | €2,100 | €700 |
| Utilities and internet | €180 | €60 |
| Shared groceries | €300 | €100 |

That's €860 each per month, which over six months comes to €5,160. Hold that figure in euros, and convert it only when you're planning what you need sent from home.

If you were budgeting at 1.1 dollars to the euro, that's about $946 a month, but that dollar figure never gets shown to your flatmates. **The shared record and your personal budget are two different documents**, and keeping them separate is the single most useful habit for managing money abroad.

## Common questions

**A flatmate wants to pay in their own currency.**
Pick one currency and have everyone work in it. The currency the rent is charged in is the natural choice. If someone does pay from another currency, record the amount that actually arrived locally. Transfer fees conventionally sit with the sender.

**What if something is still unsettled when I leave?**
Finish it before you fly. International transfers after the fact cost enough that on small balances the fee can exceed the debt. If something genuinely has to remain open, consolidate it with someone you'll see again, or transfer the balance to someone still there.

**My parents pay the rent directly. How do I record it?**
As though you paid it. What matters for settlement is whose share it counts against, not whose account it came from. Any accounting between you and your parents belongs somewhere else entirely.

**Does this apply to a short language course?**
For a month or so, treat it like a trip. Settle everything after you get home and don't bother opening a local account. This approach starts paying off when the stay runs past six months and your life has genuinely relocated.
