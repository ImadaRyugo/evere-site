A trip's spending doesn't begin on the day you leave. The hotel was booked two months ago, the flights the week after that, and someone quietly put the rental car on their own card. On plenty of trips, more money moves before departure than during it.

Pre-trip spending is also the part that goes missing when you settle up afterwards. Dinner in a restaurant leaves your wallet, so you remember it. A card charge from two months ago has been forgotten by the person who paid it.

## 🏨 Build the list before you leave, not after

If you wait until you're home to ask "wait, how much was the hotel again?", you end up digging through confirmation emails. And because different people paid for different things, the digging happens once per person.

The fix is unglamorous: **whoever confirms a booking records the amount the same day**. Not a memory exercise the week before departure — one entry the moment the payment notification lands.

You only need three things per entry: who paid, what it was for, and who it's for. Those differ by line item. The hotel is for everyone, the rental car might only be for the people who wanted a car, so decide the "who it's for" part while the booking is still fresh.

## 🎫 Keep individually booked travel out of the split

If everyone booked their own flights, those flights are not part of the settlement. Each person paid their own share, and that transaction is already closed.

Mixing them in breaks the arithmetic. Someone will point out that Chris paid $140 more for the same flight and suggest the group absorb the difference. That isn't splitting a bill, it's a subsidy. It's a perfectly reasonable thing to do, but **treat it as a separate agreement**, decided out loud, rather than folding it into the shared pot.

Different departure cities, late bookings, a friend who couldn't commit until the last minute — the reasons vary, but **individually booked means individually paid** is the cleanest default. If everyone is on the same flight anyway, having one person book all of it is usually both cheaper and easier to settle.

## 🛏️ When people aren't in the same room

Four of you take two rooms, but a twin and a triple don't cost the same per head. Split the total accommodation cost evenly and whoever ended up in the cheaper room subsidises the other one.

The simplest fix is to **record each room as its own expense**, shared only by the people who slept in it. An even split then produces the right answer on its own. If the booking came through as a single line with no breakdown, use the per-night rates from the hotel's own price list to build a ratio.

## 🧮 A worked four-person example

Alex books the accommodation for $960. Bo puts the $240 rental car on their card. Flights were booked individually, so they're out of scope.

| | Paid | Owes | Net |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alex | $960 | $300 | +$660 |
| Bo | $240 | $300 | −$60 |
| Chris | $0 | $300 | −$300 |
| Dana | $0 | $300 | −$300 |

Each share is (960 + 240) ÷ 4 = $300. Positive balances receive, negative balances pay, and the column always sums to zero, so you can check it: 660 = 60 + 300 + 300.

Notice that Bo fronted $240 and still ends up paying another $60. That surprises people every time, but it's correct: **fronting money doesn't automatically make you a net creditor**.

## 🤝 One transfer each when you get home

In the example above, Bo, Chris and Dana each send Alex one payment. Three transfers total, one per person.

Do it line item by line item instead — split the hotel four ways, then split the car four ways, then send the money back separately — and you double the number of transfers while losing track of which ones have actually gone through. ## ❓ Common questions

**How should we handle a cancellation fee?**
Unless the person who booked made a genuine mistake, everyone who was going to travel shares it. The exception is someone dropping out for their own reasons close to the date, in which case it lands on them. Agree the rule while you're still planning, not when the fee already exists.

**What about bookings paid with points or miles?**
Leaving them out of the split causes the least friction, because no cash left anyone's account. If you do want to count them, check together what the same dates would have cost in cash and record that figure. Announcing "that one was on miles" after the trip is the version that starts arguments.

**Someone got an early-bird rate. Do they keep the saving?**
They don't need to. If you only ever record what was actually paid, the saving is automatically shared by everyone. The same rule covers the trip where somebody booked badly and paid over the odds, which is why recording actual amounts stays consistent.
