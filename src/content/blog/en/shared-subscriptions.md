Subscriptions don't cause arguments because they're expensive. They cause arguments because each one is small enough to feel silly asking about. Too small to mention, so nobody mentions it, and the charges quietly pile up on whoever's card is on file.

Moving in together multiplies this. Streaming, music, cloud storage, a retail membership. Two people using them, one card paying for them.

## 📺 Put a number on the imbalance

It's easier to see with real amounts, so here's a model case.

| Service (example) | Monthly | Card on file |
|---|---|---|
| Streaming A | $15.49 | Alex |
| Streaming B | $11.99 | Alex |
| Music (family plan) | $16.99 | Sam |
| Cloud storage | $9.99 | Alex |
| Retail membership (monthly) | $12.98 | Alex |
| Total | $67.44 | |

Alex is carrying $50.45 a month and Sam $16.99. Split evenly that's $33.72 each, so Sam owes Alex $16.73.

The problem isn't the $16.73. It's that the $16.73 repeats. Over a year that's $200.76. An imbalance that size would be obvious on a trip, but spread across twelve months in small pieces, neither person notices.

## 🧩 Decide what counts as shared first

Before you decide how to split, decide what's on the list.

Keep the test simple: **would cancelling it inconvenience both of you?** Cancel the streaming service and neither of you can watch, so it's shared. A game subscription only one of you plays, or a reading app for the commute, doesn't affect the other person. That's personal.

Family plans are the awkward case. If the two of you are the only ones on the music family plan, treat it as shared. If the six seats also cover a sibling and a couple of friends, split it by the number of people actually using it. Cover your two seats and let the account holder carry the rest as something they're doing for their family. That's cleaner than working it out after the fact.

## ⚖️ Three ways to divide them

Once the list is set, pick a method.

**Spreading the cards around** removes settlement entirely. Assign services to each person so the totals roughly match, and there's no monthly step at all. The catch is that totals rarely match exactly, and you end up with a residual gap like the one above. While the gap stays small, this is fine.

**One person pays everything and you settle monthly** keeps management simple. When you want to cancel or switch plans, nobody has to work out whose account it's under. The trade-off is that skipping a settlement lets the imbalance accumulate, so it only works if you're recording.

**Paying from a joint account or card** avoids fronting money in the first place, which is the tidiest option on paper. But it means opening the account and agreeing on how it gets funded, which can be more than a couple who just moved in wants to take on. If you already run household costs through a joint account, subscriptions belong there too.

If the even split itself is the problem because you earn different amounts, [splitting by income](/en/blog/income-based-split-couples/) covers how to set the ratio.

## 🔁 Why the logging stops

Subscription tracking doesn't fail because it's tedious. It fails because the amount never changes. There's no urgency the way there is with a trip expense you'll forget by tomorrow, so it slides, and then it's gone.

The fix is to attach logging to a date instead of an amount. Pick payday or the last day of the month and record that month's subscriptions as a single entry. You don't need five entries for five services. One line for the total, labelled "Subscriptions (August)", carries everything a settlement needs.

In Evere, keep a separate [project](/en/docs/projects/) for household costs so subscriptions don't mix with trips and dinners, then add one entry a month. When a price changes you edit the number; otherwise it's the same few taps every time.

## ❓ FAQ

**Q. If we spread the cards around, does cancelling get complicated?**
It does. Keep a shared list of which service sits on whose account. When the person who wants to cancel isn't the account holder, the request stalls. If maintaining that list sounds like work, the one-payer-plus-monthly-settlement approach suits you better.

**Q. What about a free trial that converted to paid?**
Move it into shared costs from the month it converts. If you both agreed to keep it during the trial, it was a shared subscription from the start. If one of you started it alone and it auto-converted, talk before adding it to the shared list.

**Q. One of us barely uses a service. Still split it evenly?**
Splitting by usage isn't realistic, since you'd be counting hours watched every month. Suggest cancelling instead. If the person who wants to keep it insists, moving it to their personal costs is the fair outcome.
