Living abroad and going home for a couple of weeks changes how your wallet works. The stay is short, the account back home is awkward to use, and most of what you spend gets paid by somebody else.

The amounts are usually small. What defines the trip is that **the number of fronted payments keeps climbing while almost none of them get settled before you fly out**. The cause isn't the two currencies. It's that all the settling gets pushed into the last day.

## 🛬 The hard part is your position, not the currency

While you're home you're closer to a tourist than a resident. No local transit card, a payment app that stalls at identity verification, an account that still exists but sits nearly empty. Move around with family and friends in that state and the paying naturally drifts to them.

And they won't ask. Bringing up money with someone you only see once a year is uncomfortable on both sides. So the amounts end up recorded nowhere and the visit ends.

Which means the first thing to do isn't checking rates or picking a transfer service. It's the single habit of **writing down the amount each time somebody covers you**. How to repay can be decided later. What was spent cannot be recalled later.

## 💴 Whether your home account works changes the order

If the account is live and you can move money from it, this is easy. Repay in the home currency while you're still there, and no international transfer or conversion ever happens.

The problem case is a dormant account, or an app that won't verify you from abroad. Then reverse the order: on your first day, withdraw a lump sum in cash or hand it to a family member up front. Stop treating settlement as an end-of-trip task and prepay instead. Whatever is left over comes back to you on the way out.

When even that isn't possible, you'll be sending money from where you live. That's where rates and fees finally enter the picture, and the rule there is to send once. Several small transfers stack up fees that are large relative to what you're repaying.

## 🧮 Fix the rate at the moment of payment

The real argument on a trip home is never the amount, it's which day's rate to repay at. Say a family member covers $450 for you while the rate is 0.90 euro to the dollar.

| | When they paid | Settled three months later |
|---|---|---|
| Rate | 0.90 | 0.95 |
| $450 converted | €405 | €427.50 |

That's 450 × 0.90 = €405 against 450 × 0.95 = €427.50, a gap of €22.50. Neither is objectively correct, but with nothing agreed the person repaying looks at the cheaper figure and the person owed looks at the higher one.

The principle is to **fix the rate at the moment the payment happened**. What they actually spent was $450, and the value of that day is the debt. ## 🎁 Keep gifts and hosted meals out of it

Spending on a trip home is a mix of things that should be settled and things that shouldn't. Fail to separate them and the record turns heavy overnight.

Meals where you're the guest usually belong in the second group. Trying to split what someone laid on for you after a year apart amounts to refusing the gesture. Something you asked them to buy, or a bill they paid on your behalf, is a different kind of thing entirely.

The test isn't the size of the amount, it's **whether you asked for it**. Settle what you asked for; accept what was offered. Draw that one line and you stop deliberating over what to record.

## ✈️ Close it the day before you fly

The last day disappears into packing and travel. Finish the settling before it.

Three steps: total what people covered, agree the method, hand it over and close the record. If you noted amounts during the trip, all three take fifteen minutes. If you didn't, you start with reconstruction, and most people abandon it halfway.

Avoid promising to send it once you're back. The first week back is jet lag and work, and a transfer will lose every priority contest it enters. If it genuinely has to wait, give the other person the amount and the date in a form they can see, so the record does the chasing instead of them. For a full multi-currency trip, [splitting bills across currencies](/en/blog/split-bills-different-currencies/) covers the rest.

## ❓ Common questions

**Q. My parents say they don't want it back. Do I still record it?**
Record it, at least. You don't have to repay what somebody genuinely refuses, but knowing what they spent changes how you prepare for the next visit, and it's a useful input when you're deciding what to bring back.

**Q. Who pays the transfer fee?**
Whoever sends. The fee is a cost of the repayment method you chose, and it has nothing to do with the person who fronted the money. If you want to reduce it, send fewer times or settle in cash on your next trip.

**Q. What if I fronted money for someone while I was home?**
Same treatment. Bear in mind that receiving it after you fly back turns it into an international transfer for them. If the amount is small, offsetting it against something on your next visit is more realistic.

**Q. If family paid my bill from their account, which currency do I record?**
The one that was actually paid. Recording it converted into your everyday currency means that later, when you check the figure, you can't tell which number was the original. Conversion is a display question and can be handled afterwards.
