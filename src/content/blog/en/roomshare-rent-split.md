When three people share a place, splitting the rent three ways is what starts the arguments. Everyone pays the same, but one person has the big corner room and someone else has the small one facing the alley. Everyone notices within the first week.

To make it fair, the difference between the rooms has to become a number. The method is fairly settled: build a ratio from floor area, carve out the common space, then price whatever difference is left.

## 📐 Start with the area ratio

Take a $2,700 apartment shared by three, with private rooms of 160, 120 and 80 square feet. The ratio is 4:3:2, which adds up to 9.

| | Private room | Ratio | Rent |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alex | 160 sq ft | 4 | $1,200 |
| Bo | 120 sq ft | 3 | $900 |
| Chris | 80 sq ft | 2 | $600 |

The math is just 2,700 × 4/9 = $1,200 and so on. Compared with an even split of $900 each, Alex pays $300 more and Chris pays $300 less.

This is where most people feel something is off. Chris is getting away too cheaply. That instinct is right, because this calculation ignores the common space entirely.

## 🛋️ Split the common space evenly

The living room, kitchen, bathroom and hallway don't belong to anyone in particular. Everyone has an equal claim on them. Splitting purely by private area discounts the small-room person's access to shared space too, which isn't what anyone intended.

So divide the rent into two parts: **common space split evenly, private space split by area**.

If the common areas account for 40% of the floor plan, then $1,080 of the $2,700 is common and $1,620 is private.

| | Common (even) | Private (by area) | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alex | $360 | $720 | $1,080 |
| Bo | $360 | $540 | $900 |
| Chris | $360 | $360 | $720 |

It still adds to $2,700. The spread is narrower than the area-only figures of $1,200 / $900 / $600, and closer to what the rooms actually feel worth. Estimate the common-space share off the floor plan by eye. Whether you call it 30%, 40% or 50% doesn't change the conclusion, only how strongly the difference bites.

## ➕ Price the things area doesn't capture

Two rooms of the same size are still not the same room.

Natural light, closet space, a balcony, whether the air conditioning was already installed, distance from the bathroom. The fastest way to handle these is to say out loud what each is worth per month. South-facing is $30, balcony access is $20. Add that to the occupant's share and subtract it evenly from everyone.

What matters isn't pricing these precisely, it's **agreeing on them before you sign**. Raising it after move-in, when someone has already unpacked, turns a pricing question into a personal one. Ideally you walk the apartment together at the viewing and settle it on the spot.

Some groups draw lots for rooms. If you do, still fix the price differences first. Decide afterwards and the person holding the best room is suddenly the one arguing that the premium is too high.

## 📆 Prorate mid-month moves

When someone joins or leaves partway through a month, prorate by days.

Moving into the $720 room on August 18 means 14 of August's 31 days, so 720 × 14 ÷ 31 = $325.16. Round up or down as you like, as long as you decide which before the first invoice.

Departures are the harder problem. When one person leaves, everyone else's rent rises automatically. Three people on $2,700 pay $900 each; two people pay $1,350. Who covers the empty room until a replacement is found is the thing to settle in advance, with the departing person in the room. A rule like "one month's notice, and the person leaving covers half the vacancy until someone moves in" is easy to write down at move-in and impossible to negotiate calmly afterwards.

Security deposits are usually distributed after everyone has moved out and the damage deductions are final. Refund a departing housemate early and you have no way to claw it back when the landlord's deductions land.

## 🧾 Reuse the ratio every month

Once the ratio exists, every month is the same work. Decide once whether utilities and internet follow the same ratio or get split evenly, and from then on you're only swapping in new numbers.

Rent by area, utilities evenly is the common pairing. You'll be tempted to split utilities by usage, but there are no per-room meters, so there is nothing to measure.

## ❓ FAQ

**Q. What if we can't measure the rooms accurately?**
The dimensions on the listing's floor plan are enough. If one room is 12 by 10 and another is 9 by 10, treat that as 4:3. Chasing precision doesn't help when you're already eyeballing the common-space percentage.

**Q. Only one of us is on the lease. How do we collect?**
The leaseholder pays the landlord in full and everyone pays the leaseholder. That means they're fronting the whole rent every month, so set the internal due date just after payday and keep the collection tight.

**Q. What if someone calls it unfair later?**
Either swap rooms or reprice. Don't backdate the change, though. Applying it from next month turns the conversation into a rule for the future rather than a claim about the past, which is much easier to settle.
