While you lived together, you split rent and groceries down the middle. Now the wedding is set, and a thought creeps in: once we are married, do we still need any of this?

The answer differs from couple to couple, but one thing holds for everyone. **Getting married is one of the few moments when you can redraw how you share money from scratch.** Let the old arrangement just carry on, and the next natural chance to change it may not come for years.

## Close out the old balance when you marry

Splitting while living together nearly always leaves something unsettled. Even couples who square up at the end of each month have the days between their last settle-up and the wedding hanging in the air.

Clear that first. Even if the amount is small, getting it to zero matters, because **when "what is still owed" gets mixed into "what we do from now on," neither conversation moves.**

You can close it out before you decide whether anything changes. In fact, before is better. Once nobody owes anybody, writing new rules on a blank page is a far calmer conversation.

## Whether to stop splitting has nothing to do with the number of accounts

The picture of marriage as one shared wallet is persistent. In practice, plenty of married couples keep separate accounts, open a joint one for the bills, and share only living costs.

The question worth asking is not how many accounts you will have. It is **whether you will still need to tell whose money is whose.**

If both incomes go into one account and everything, bills and personal spending alike, comes out of it, settling up mostly disappears. If paychecks keep landing in separate accounts and one of you covers rent while the other covers groceries, an imbalance builds every month, and settling up stays. How couples with separate finances keep that running is in [why you need a monthly settlement day](/en/blog/monthly-settlement-day/).

Neither is the right answer. The comparison of splitting methods is in [how to split living costs when you move in together](/en/blog/cohabitation-expense-split/), but at marriage the question is not which method is best. It is which way the two of you want to live.

## Draw a line around pre-marriage money before you mix it

The money each of you had before the wedding is where couples hesitate most. Savings from single life, the rest of a student loan, a car still being paid off.

Whether any of it joins the household is yours to decide together. If you mix it, though, **write down the date and the amount when you do.** One of you put more savings into the down payment; you agreed the household would cover one person's loan payments. It feels natural at the time, and a few years later nobody remembers who put in what.

The same thinking works for a large gift from parents. How to treat that between the two of you is in [parents helping with your shared budget](/en/blog/parental-support-couple-budget/).

## Line up the account names in the order money flows

Marriage brings a wave of paperwork. If one of you changes your name, bank accounts and cards need updating too.

Use the moment to tidy the paths your living costs travel. Start with what leaves every month without fail: rent or mortgage, utilities, phone, insurance. If you are moving autopay to a joint account, moving these first removes most of the monthly imbalance in one go.

Subscriptions and hobby cards can wait. The amounts are small and personal and shared items blur together easily, so rushing them makes the line sloppy. For shared subscriptions, see [who pays for streaming and music](/en/blog/shared-subscriptions/).

## Stop splitting, but keep the record

Even couples who merge everything and stop settling up get something from a record. Not a record for settling, but one for the household.

When you were splitting, you had to record everything or you could not settle, so it happened by habit. Take settling away and one reason to record disappears. Then, suddenly, nobody can see how much the two of you spend each month.

What changes when you marry is how you divide money, not the habit of talking about it. Not letting that habit go on the day you stop splitting is probably the switch that matters most.
