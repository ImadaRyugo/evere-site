The family car, the holiday house a grandparent built, the piece of land that came down from a parent. Anything a family owns together comes with upkeep attached.

And upkeep arrives in the years nobody uses it. **Usage differs between households by a factor of several, while the bill turns up identical every year** — which is the whole difficulty.

## 🏖️ The same bill, wildly different use

Take the holiday house. The sibling who lives nearby uses it monthly. The one two hundred miles away manages twice a year. The one living abroad comes once every few years.

Split the upkeep in three and the sibling abroad pays annually for something they don't use. Split it purely by nights stayed and a year where nobody goes has no source of funds at all.

Both objections are legitimate, which is why **the costs need splitting in two before the conversation starts.** Once they are, most of the disagreement evaporates.

## 🧾 Costs of owning, costs of using

Upkeep on a shared asset is two different things wearing one label.

| Type | Examples | Who carries it |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed | Property tax, insurance, service charge, annual test | Divided by ownership share |
| Variable | Utilities, cleaning, fuel, consumables | Divided by how much each used |

Fixed costs accrue whether anyone shows up or not. They are the price of owning the thing, so ownership shares should carry them. Variable costs go to whoever generated them. The two-tier idea is the same one behind [treating camping gear as depreciation](/en/blog/camping-gear-fair-split/).

## 🧮 Running the two tiers

Three households share a holiday house. Annual costs are $2,400 fixed and $1,200 variable. The nights came to 20, 12 and 8, forty in total.

$1,200 across forty nights is $30 a night.

| | Fixed (three ways) | Variable (nights × $30) | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Eldest (20 nights) | $800 | $600 | $1,400 |
| Middle (12 nights) | $800 | $360 | $1,160 |
| Youngest (8 nights) | $800 | $240 | $1,040 |
| Total | $2,400 | $1,200 | $3,600 |

An even split would have been $1,200 each. The actual range runs from $1,400 down to $1,040. A $360 spread may look modest next to the difference in use. What matters more is that **nobody gets a full invoice for a year they never turned up.**

## 🔑 Price the work of whoever manages it

Every shared asset has someone managing it: clearing the gutters, taking the car for its test, booking the gardener, holding the keys.

**That work isn't free.** Left alone, the person who lives closest absorbs all of it, and absorbs it invisibly for years. The same structure appears in [splitting a parent's care costs between siblings](/en/blog/sibling-elder-care-costs/), and so does the remedy: put a price on it and add it to the cost side.

Four arrangements a year at $50 each is $200. The figure needs no justification beyond everyone agreeing to it.

## 🚪 Write down the exit terms early

Shared assets cause the least trouble while they're being maintained and the most when someone wants out.

Can a sibling who says they'll never use it again actually leave? Do they get back what they've paid in upkeep? How is a sale divided? If the deeds are still in a parent's name, what does inheritance do to all of this?

Settling every one of those before you start is unrealistic, but **once something is decided, write it down.** A verbal agreement ceases to exist one generation later. On a sale, the principle is simply to reuse the ratio of what people put in, because that is the version you can explain.
