You booked a table for ten and eight people showed up. The kitchen prepared ten covers, and the restaurant is charging for ten. What to do about that gap is the part of organising that nobody enjoys.

In most cases the gap goes unbilled and **the organiser swallows it**. Collect $50 from everyone present, then quietly put the missing $100 on your own card. That's how the evening usually ends.

## 🚫 Absorb it silently and it happens again

Covering the difference does keep the peace. Nobody has an uncomfortable conversation, so it looks like the generous call.

The trouble is that it carries straight over to the next event. Nobody learns that cancelling costs money, so the same number of people drop out next time. And by then the organiser is somebody else, who absorbs the same amount again.

So whether to bill it isn't a question about tonight's money. It's a question about whether this group can keep meeting for years without burning through its volunteers.

## 📅 Publish the free-cancellation date up front

Charging a cancellation fee feels awkward almost entirely because nobody mentioned it in advance. Raising it for the first time on the day sounds like a rule invented on the spot.

One line at the invitation stage is enough. Copying the venue's own policy is the easiest version: if they say free until three days before and full price after that, say exactly that. **Presenting it as the restaurant's terms rather than your own rule** keeps you from looking like the strict one.

It also helps to set your RSVP deadline to the same day you have to confirm numbers with the venue. Then the logic is self-evident: changes after the headcount goes in are changes that cost money.

## 💬 What to say when the message arrives

Decide in advance what you'll reply, and it stops being a decision you make while annoyed. It doesn't need to be long, just factual.

For illness or a work emergency, lead with concern, then add that the numbers already went to the restaurant. You don't have to rule on the money right there. "I'll send the totals round afterwards" is a good place to stop, because settling amounts while feelings are running is how these go wrong.

After the event, send the figures to everyone in the same format. Invoicing the absentee privately reads as chasing; a line for them inside a group summary reads as a fact.

## ⚖️ Charge them, re-split, or halve it

There are three shapes this can take: the no-show pays their full share, the attendees re-split the whole bill, or the two sides meet in the middle.

**Charging the no-show is the most defensible.** Their RSVP is what set the headcount, and the restaurant bought food on the strength of it. There's no principled reason for the people who did turn up to pay more.

Re-splitting among attendees is, in practice, usually what happens when nobody could face raising it. That said, if you didn't collect money in advance, chasing an absent person for $50 may genuinely not be worth it. In that case, choosing this option deliberately beats issuing an invoice you'll never collect.

Halving suits the unavoidable cases, like illness. It avoids assigning blame while still recording that a cost occurred, which makes it easy to live with in a group that meets regularly.

## 🧮 Ten booked, two absent

A $50 set menu booked for ten, with two no-shows. The restaurant charges $500 either way.

| Approach | Each of the 8 present | Each of the 2 absent | Organiser is out |
|---|---|---|---|
| No-show pays | $50 | $50 | $0 |
| Attendees re-split | $62.50 | $0 | $0 |
| Split the difference | $56.25 | $25 | $0 |

All three add up to $500: 8 × 50 + 2 × 50, or 8 × 62.50, or 8 × 56.25 + 2 × 25.

The thing worth noticing is that **every one of these leaves the organiser at zero**. The version where the organiser pays isn't on this table at all. It isn't one of the options, it's what happens when nobody picks one.

## ❓ Common questions

**They say it's unfair to pay for food they didn't eat.**
Show them the venue's policy. Once it's clear the number came from the restaurant charging for a booking past its cancellation deadline, and not from your judgement, most people accept it. Arguments happen when the source of the rule looks like your opinion.

**Does collecting money in advance solve this?**
It solves the collection problem almost entirely. The trade-off is that you're now holding other people's money and have to account for it. Reserving prepayment for large events, or ones where attendance is genuinely uncertain, is the practical middle ground.

**Does someone who skipped the second venue owe a cancellation fee for the first?**
No. If they came to the first one, they didn't cancel anything. The fee follows the booking headcount, which has nothing to do with what they did afterwards. [Treating the second venue as its own event](/en/blog/drinking-party-organizer-guide/) prevents this confusion entirely.

**What if the organiser is the one who can't make it?**
The organiser is a participant like anyone else, so the same rule applies to them. What you do want to avoid is nobody on site handling the bill, so nominate a substitute to collect and pay, and share the recorded amounts with them beforehand.
