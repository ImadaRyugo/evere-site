Four friends head to the mountains. They share a lodge and a car, yet the money each of them spends is wildly different. One brings their own board. One rents everything down to the jacket. One is a beginner booked into a lesson on day one. And one has no plans to ski at all, just the hot tub and the café.

Divide the whole trip by four and the non-skier ends up paying for lift passes. Make everything pay-your-own and nobody remembers who covered the lodge or the gas. **Draw the line by type of cost before you go, and you avoid both mistakes.**

## 🎿 What everyone shares, and what's personal

Ski trip costs are easier to sort than most. Almost everything falls into one of two buckets: it's either for skiing, or it's for the trip itself.

| Cost | How to split | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Lodging | Everyone | Everyone sleeps there, skiing or not |
| Gas, tolls, parking | Everyone in the car | Getting there is part of the trip |
| Winter gear for the car | Everyone in the car | Part of the cost of driving on snow |
| Lift passes | Each skier | Clear split between users and non-users |
| Rentals, lessons | Each person | Everyone needs a different amount |
| Lunch on the mountain | Each person | People eat in different places at different times |

The only row people argue about is winter gear for the car. Rental companies sometimes include snow tires or chains and sometimes charge extra, depending on the region and the company. If it's an extra charge, treat it like gas: everyone in the car shares it. The non-skier rides the same snowy road, so it's their cost too.

How to split the car itself, including whether the driver should pay less, is covered in [Rental Car Trips: Should Non-Drivers Pay the Same?](/en/blog/rental-car-trip-costs/)

## 🎟️ Lift passes only need tracking when one person buys them all

If everyone buys their own pass at the ticket window, there's nothing to settle. The trouble starts when one person buys everyone's passes online, which is common because buying ahead is often cheaper and skips the morning line.

Whoever buys them is fronting the money. And **if people ski different numbers of days**, the math drifts. The friend who's spending day two at the spa doesn't need a two-day pass. Confirm who needs how many days before you buy.

Refund rules for weather closures differ from resort to resort. If you're buying for the group, check the refund conditions on the checkout page so you're not scrambling to explain it on the day.

## 🧑‍🏫 Beginners and experts spend in opposite ways

Beginners pay for rentals and lessons but spend less time on the lifts. Experts own their gear and pay no rental fees, then ride from first chair to last.

Whether each person gets their money's worth from the pass varies, but almost nobody adjusts for that when settling up. Some groups are tempted to chip in for the beginner because their trip costs more. But **rentals and lessons are choices each person makes for themselves**. Keep them out of the shared pot and you'll avoid arguments later.

If a friend lends you their own board, you'll probably want to give something back instead of a rental fee. The thinking in [Camping Costs: Fair Splits When Someone Brings the Gear](/en/blog/camping-gear-fair-split/) applies directly.

## ♨️ The non-skier pays for the lodge and the ride

It's not unusual for one person on a ski trip not to ski. Some come for the snow and the hot springs; some are recovering from an injury and just want to be there.

Their share is lodging and transport, nothing more. They didn't get a lift pass, so there's no reason to charge them for one. If dinner is included in the lodging price, that stays a shared cost as well.

On the other hand, whatever the non-skier spends alone during the day, a spa visit or a café, is their own expense. It doesn't come out of the group's money.

## 🧮 A two-day trip for four

A owns a board and drives, B rents everything, C is a beginner taking a lesson, and D doesn't ski. The amounts are only for illustration.

The shared costs are the $600 lodge that B booked and the $160 in gas and tolls that A paid. C bought three two-day lift passes online for $420. Everyone paid for their own rentals and lessons.

| | Lodge | Travel | Lift pass | Share | Paid | Balance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | $150 | $40 | $140 | $330 | $160 | Pays $170 |
| B | $150 | $40 | $140 | $330 | $600 | Gets $270 |
| C | $150 | $40 | $140 | $330 | $420 | Gets $90 |
| D | $150 | $40 | $0 | $190 | $0 | Pays $190 |

The shares add up to $1,180, matching what was paid. Three transfers settle it: D sends B $190, A sends B $80, and A sends C $90.

**D, who didn't ski, pays $140 less than the others.** That's exactly one lift pass. If someone skis only one day, run the same calculation with the price of a one-day pass.

## ❓ Common questions

**Q. What if someone joins for just one day?**
They don't pay for lodging, so they cover transport and that day's lift pass. If they get there on their own, they don't share transport either. Choosing participants per expense handles people who join partway without any extra math.

**Q. Someone gets hurt on day one and can't ski day two. Now what?**
If the pass can't be refunded, it stays their cost. It's rough luck, but re-splitting it means someone else pays for a pass they never used. That's why it's worth checking the refund rules first.

**Q. What about someone who buys a jacket or goggles on the trip?**
They keep what they buy, so it's a personal expense. Unlike a rental, they'll use it on the next trip too, so it naturally stays out of the trip costs.

**Q. The rooms are split by gender and priced differently. How do we handle that?**
Either everyone pays for their own room, or you divide the whole lodging bill by headcount. If the price difference is small, split by headcount; if it's large, splitting by room is usually seen as fairer.
