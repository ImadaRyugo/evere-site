For an ordinary group dinner, there's only one source of money: what each person pays. Divide the restaurant bill by the headcount and the accounting is done.

The office holiday party doesn't work like that. Your manager hands you a contribution, the company has a budget, and the raffle prizes were bought last week on your own card. **Money is coming in from several places and going out to several places**, which is exactly why the numbers stop adding up once the night is over.

Choosing the venue, running the evening, and handling odd amounts are covered in [The Group Dinner Organizer's Guide to Splitting the Bill](/en/blog/drinking-party-organizer-guide/). This piece looks only at the money that's specific to the holiday party: everything that isn't the attendees' own fee.

## A manager's contribution looks different depending on where it goes

When a manager chips in for the party, the usual courtesy is to thank them by name at the start of the evening without announcing the amount.

There are three broad ways to use the money: lower everyone's fee for the dinner, spend it on prizes, or put it toward the after-party. None of them is wrong, but they look very different to the people attending.

Lowering the fee is the most even-handed. But a $200 contribution spread across 20 people only takes $10 off each fee, and few people notice a change that small. **The gesture dissolves into the fee and becomes invisible.** That's the weakness of this option.

Spending it on prizes makes it visible. Call the best prize "the manager's prize" and everyone can see whose money bought what. You show gratitude without revealing the amount, which fits neatly with the habit of not announcing it.

If it goes to the after-party, remember that people who leave after dinner get nothing from it. The money was given to the whole party, so if only some people will benefit, it's worth checking with the person who gave it.

## What about the contributor's own fee?

The awkward question is whether the manager who chipped in also pays the regular fee. Some people expect to pay both. Others assume their contribution already covers their own seat.

Don't guess. When you receive the money, ask: "Should I still collect your regular fee?" It's an easy question to answer on the spot, and it saves you an awkward explanation later.

## Ask about the company budget's conditions first

If the company or the department is paying part of the bill, the money often comes with rules. Does it cover employees only, or contractors and temps too? Is there a per-person cap? What name and description should go on the receipt?

Ask finance or your manager before you book. Getting a receipt reissued after the event is a hassle, and if someone turns out not to be covered, you'll have to recalculate their fee without the subsidy.

When someone isn't covered, their fee ends up higher than everyone else's. That's company policy, not something the organizer needs to feel bad about, but telling the person beforehand saves an awkward explanation while you're collecting.

## Prizes usually become the organizer's out-of-pocket cost

Prizes have to be bought before the party, so the organizer usually pays for them personally. Then the night itself is so busy that by the time you collect money, you've forgotten all about them.

The reliable fix is to **photograph the receipt and record the amount the moment you buy**. Decide before you buy whether prizes come out of the manager's contribution, the company budget, or a top-up to the fee. Looking for a funding source after the purchase tends to end with the organizer covering the gap.

Nobody charges absent colleagues for prizes. If the number of prizes stays the same when attendance drops, the cost falls on the people who came. For what to do about the restaurant charge when someone cancels late, see [Who Pays When Someone No-Shows a Group Booking?](/en/blog/cancellation-fee-who-pays/)

## Running the numbers for a 20-person party

Take a $50-a-head set menu for 20 people and $150 in raffle prizes. The manager contributes $200, and the company covers $20 per person for everyone.

The total cost is $1,000 for the restaurant plus $150 for prizes: $1,150. Take away the company's $400 and $750 is left.

| How the contribution is used | Fee per person | Where the contribution goes |
|---|---|---|
| No contribution | $37.50 | — |
| Lower everyone's fee | $27.50 | $10 off each fee |
| Prizes and after-party | $30.00 | $150 on prizes, $50 to the after-party |

Lowering the fee: $750 minus the $200 contribution leaves $550, divided by 20 is $27.50. Using it for prizes and the after-party: the prizes are paid from the contribution, so the restaurant bill minus the company's share, $600, divided by 20 is $30.

**The difference between the two approaches is $2.50 a person.** At that point, choosing the option that makes the gesture visible is a perfectly reasonable call.

## Keep separate records for each source

Holiday party accounts get hard to explain because fees, contributions, the company budget, and prize money all end up in one wallet. Keep them apart in your records and you can answer anyone's question with a number.

In Evere, record whatever the contribution and the company budget didn't cover as one expense, with everyone as [participants](/en/docs/expenses/) and an equal split. Put the amounts and uses of the contribution and the budget in the memo, and next year's organizer can take it over as is.

## Common questions

**Q. How should I thank the manager afterward?**
Thanking them on the night plus a quick word from the organizer the next working day is plenty. No gift is needed. If the money went to prizes, mentioning what you bought is a nice touch.

**Q. What if some of the contribution is left over?**
It usually goes to the after-party or next year's event, but let the person who gave it know rather than deciding alone. You could refund it through the fee, but split 20 ways it comes to very little and isn't worth the effort.

**Q. If someone isn't covered by the company budget, should everyone else make up the difference?**
There's no rule. Smaller groups sometimes even out the fees so everyone pays the same. That's a separate decision from the company's policy, though, so ask the group rather than deciding it for them.

**Q. What if we bought too many prizes?**
Save them for the next event or hand them out at the organizer's discretion. Billing anyone for leftover prizes is hard, so set the prize budget as a hard cap in advance.
