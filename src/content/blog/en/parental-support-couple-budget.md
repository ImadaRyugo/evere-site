Live with someone long enough and money arrives from a parent. A deposit on a flat, help replacing the car, something towards the wedding, a hand when a baby comes. Whatever the size, this kind of money gets placed somewhere slightly separate inside a household budget.

If nobody decides where, the separateness comes back later.

## Help from parents is asymmetric in a way earnings aren't

Two salaries come from two jobs. They can differ wildly in size and still be symmetric in origin. Money from a parent isn't: it comes from one side's family.

That asymmetry quietly moves the fairness line. You can split the rent down the middle while 60% of the deposit came from one set of parents, in which case the asset underneath you is not split down the middle at all. Handle the monthly costs meticulously and **the largest number in your joint life gets settled without anyone discussing it.**

Property money is the clearest case. If $50,000 of a deposit is $30,000 from one side's parents and $20,000 from joint savings, then family money to joint money is 60:40 — and if the joint savings count as half each, one side has contributed $40,000 of the $50,000, or 80%. Whose name goes on the deeds is a legal question. How the two of you regard it is a separate one, and it needs answering.

## "My parents paid for that" changes the balance of power

Nobody has to say it aloud for the source of the money to carry weight.

The person whose family helped tends to lead on how it gets used. The other tends to hold back. Neither is acting badly. Still, choosing furniture, choosing where to live, the ratio of deference to assertion shifts a little.

The awkward part is that this stays invisible while things go well. Nobody raises it. Then an unrelated argument comes along and **"my parents were the ones who paid for it" turns up in a conversation it has no business being in.** By that point it isn't about money at all.

Saying it out loud early is safer. This money is ours, or this money is an asset from one family. Either answer works. Only the unanswered version causes trouble.

## A one-off and an ongoing subsidy are different things

Help comes in two shapes: a lump sum that lands once, and a continuing flow — help with the rent, a monthly transfer, groceries that keep arriving.

The lump sum is an asset question. It went into something identifiable, so it can be traced later, and deciding how to treat it is relatively easy.

The continuing one becomes a living-costs question, and once it is inside your living costs it stops being visible. If $300 a month is arriving, that is part of your disposable income. Live as though it doesn't exist and you are fine when it stops. Build your life on it and the budget fails the month it ends. If you receive ongoing help, **work out once what your budget looks like without it.** Doing the sum is enough to show you how dependent you actually are.

The design of the living costs themselves is covered in [splitting expenses when you live together](/en/blog/cohabitation-expense-split/); treating family help as income sitting outside that structure keeps the picture clear.

## Into the joint pot, or kept personal?

There are two workable choices: put the money into the joint budget, or treat it as the personal asset of whoever received it.

Put it in the joint pot and it belongs to both of you. You both decide how it gets spent, and from then on the fact that it came from one set of parents drops out of the decision-making. **If it can't drop out, it shouldn't have gone in.**

Keep it personal and the existing split carries on untouched. The recipient holds it as their own savings and doesn't spend it on shared things. That reads as cold, and it has the fewest moving parts to fall out over later.

Which fits depends on how your money works already. If you run a joint account of the kind described in [saving towards a goal together](/en/blog/shared-savings-goal/), the first suits you. If everything beyond the bills is separate, the second is the natural one.

## Money you'll never repay still needs a record

Help from parents usually comes with no expectation of repayment, which is exactly why no record gets kept.

**But not repaying it and pretending it never happened are different things.** Whose parents, when, how much, and what for. Those four facts are the whole record. [Keep it in a separate project](/en/docs/projects/) in Evere and it stays out of the monthly settling-up while remaining findable.

The record earns its keep somewhere other than between the two of you: a future conversation with siblings about an estate, a mortgage application asking about contributions, or a relationship that changes shape. In all of those, a record finishes the conversation faster than memory does.

If raising the subject at all feels heavy, start from [the script for talking about money with your partner](/en/blog/money-talk-with-partner/) instead. Deciding how family help is treated isn't a negotiation over amounts. It's confirming what you both already assume.
