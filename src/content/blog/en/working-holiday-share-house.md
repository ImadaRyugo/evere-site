When the money stops adding up in a working-holiday share house, the cause is rarely the language or a slip in the arithmetic. It's that **rent runs weekly, bills run monthly, and wages arrive fortnightly, all at the same time**.

Try to force those three onto one rhythm and something breaks every month. It settles far faster if you build a setup that tolerates the different rhythms instead of flattening them.

## 🎒 How you count turns straight into how much you pay

In a weekly let, rent is quoted per week. Treat four of those weeks as a month and the error compounds until it's substantial by the end of a year.

Here's a room at A$250 a week.

| How it's counted | Per month | Per year |
|---|---|---|
| 250 × 4 weeks, called a month | A$1,000 | A$12,000 |
| 250 × 52 weeks, divided by 12 | A$1,083.33 | A$13,000 |

The real annual figure is 250 × 52 = A$13,000, which is A$1,083.33 a month. Stacking up four-week months gives A$12,000, a gap of A$1,000. The reason is the obvious one: a year holds 52 weeks, not 48.

So if you budget monthly, don't multiply the weekly rent by four. **Multiply by 52 and divide by 12** for the real monthly figure. The same gap, left uncorrected in a shared budget, is what produces the recurring feeling of being slightly short every month.

## 📅 Run rent and living costs on separate cycles

Rent is weekly, power and internet arrive per bill, groceries happen whenever. Forcing all three into a single settlement date is what falls apart.

The workable version is to fix rent entirely and pool only what's left. Everyone pays the landlord or head tenant directly each week. Shared utilities and household basics go into a common record and get cleared once a month. **Taking rent out of the split** shrinks the amount you're tracking so sharply that the settlement itself becomes a five-minute job.

Whether to pool groceries depends on how many people there are and how long they're staying. In a room that turns over every three months, everyone buying their own leaves less to argue about. Past six months together, pooling is genuinely cheaper.

## 🧾 Keep the bond away from living costs

The deposit you hand over on arrival is a different kind of money entirely. Mix it into the monthly record and, on the day you move out, nobody can say who is owed what.

Handling it takes three facts and nothing more: who paid how much, which account it's held in, and when it comes back to whom. If you've taken over a room from a previous tenant or you're subletting, check on day one whether **the person you paid and the person who refunds you are the same person**. A mismatch there is unfixable in the week you're leaving.

## 💱 Don't blend your home currency with the local one

The other reason the picture goes blurry is that two accounts are live at once. Flights and visa fees came out of the home account, daily life runs on local currency, and savings sit in both.

The common mistake is converting every local expense back home at that day's rate as you record it. Because each line uses a different rate, the total at the end means nothing. Record living costs in local currency and let the home currency stay a separate conversation. Settle with housemates in local currency only, and use conversion purely inside your own budget.

## 🔁 Build it assuming people leave

Assume the faces in the house change within six months. Tie a rule to a person and it has to be rebuilt the day that person flies home.

The trick that survives handover is tying decisions to rooms instead. Not "Sam pays the electricity" but "whoever has the back room holds the electricity account". With the amounts recorded, you can show a new arrival what last month actually cost rather than guessing at it.

When someone leaves mid-period, count the final stretch in weeks rather than days if the rent is weekly. The tenancy is cut in weeks, so matching it gives you something to point at. ## ❓ Common questions

**Q. The rent includes bills. What still gets split?**
Leave anything already included alone. Only what's genuinely extra goes into the shared record: an internet upgrade, cleaning supplies, a gas bottle. For any line item where inclusion is unclear, asking on the first day is the cheapest possible time to ask.

**Q. What if a housemate only deals in cash?**
Separate the record from the method of payment. Cash changing hands settles perfectly well as long as the amount gets written down. The problem was never cash, it's a handover that exists in nobody's memory.

**Q. My pay is fortnightly and it doesn't line up with rent day.**
Set aside four weeks of rent up front. Pay cycles and rent cycles will never align, so stop trying and take the rent out of your income first. Living on what remains makes the mismatch a non-event.

**Q. When should I settle up before flying home?**
Finish it a week before you leave. The final week disappears into packing and closing accounts. Deciding who keeps the shared furniture and appliances at the same time lets you have the money conversation once instead of three times.
