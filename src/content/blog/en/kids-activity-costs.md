You both work, and you split living costs by income. Rent and groceries have run fine that way. Then swim lessons arrive, then piano, then someone mentions tutoring.

Before the amounts, the doubts arrive. Should these costs follow the same ratio as everything else? If one of you pushed for an activity, should that parent carry more of it? **Kids' costs are money where the uncertainty about splitting grows just as fast as the amounts do.**

## 🎹 Is the living-costs ratio enough for kids' costs?

In short, the ratio itself can often stay the same. The children belong to both of you, and the reason for splitting by income is no different from rent or groceries. How to set that ratio is covered in [splitting expenses by income ratio](/en/blog/income-based-split-couples/).

What should change is not the ratio but where the costs live. Mixed into general living costs, kids' expenses disappear from view. You cannot tell whether groceries went up or lesson fees did.

**Keep the same ratio, but give kids' costs a bucket of their own.** That alone lets you both look at the same numbers when costs start climbing, which they will.

## 📈 Activity costs climb as the kids get older

Kids' costs have a feature other living costs do not: they grow year after year on their own.

Here is one two-earner family splitting activity fees 60:40 by income.

| Stage | Activities | Per month | 60:40 split |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kindergarten | Swim lessons | $120 | $72 / $48 |
| 2nd grade | Swim + piano | $260 | $156 / $104 |
| 4th grade | Piano + tutoring | $500 | $300 / $200 |

In four years the monthly cost has more than quadrupled. With the ratio unchanged, the lower earner's share went from $48 to $200, a number nobody pictured when the living-costs ratio was set.

So treat a big change in kids' costs as a moment to revisit the ratio. Agree that **the month a new activity starts is the month you look at the ratio again**, and nobody has to hunt for a way to bring it up.

## 🙋 Decide how to pay when you decide to start

Activity costs cause friction most often when you agreed to start something without talking about payment.

A common unspoken rule is that whoever suggested it pays. Dad pushed for soccer, so Dad pays for soccer. It sounds fair, but over time it makes it harder for the other parent to weigh in on the activity. The opinion of the parent who isn't paying inevitably sounds weaker.

Decide together whether to start, and once you do, pay from the kids' bucket at your usual ratio. **Separating who suggested it from who pays for it** keeps the kids' activities something you both get to talk about.

## 🎒 It's the extras, not the fees, that shake the budget

Monthly fees are the same every month, so you can plan for them. The extras are what shake the budget.

Recital fees and costumes, summer camps and away tournaments, new instruments and gear, exam and grading fees. Depending on the activity, these can approach a full year of lesson fees. And they tend to land on whichever parent happened to have the card out that day.

Record each extra as it happens and put it in the same kids' bucket as the fees. If whoever paid just absorbs it quietly, a year later one parent has paid hundreds more without anyone noticing. Once a year, look at next year's recitals and camps together and estimate the extras, so the big ones are not a surprise.

Kids' costs are not something one parent carries alone. **Give them their own bucket and look at the same numbers together.** With that in place, changing the ratio or adding an activity becomes a much shorter conversation.
