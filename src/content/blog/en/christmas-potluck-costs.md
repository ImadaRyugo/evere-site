A potluck feels easier than splitting a bill. Everyone brings a dish, and nobody has to collect money afterward.

But look at the table in dollar terms and the gaps are real. A preordered cake: $48. A bag of chips grabbed on the way: $9. Both count as "bringing something," yet **one person spent about five times as much as the other**. And if the same friends meet every year, it tends to be the same person who ends up on cake duty.

## 🎄 Closing the gap without killing the potluck

Switching to a flat fee the moment it feels uneven takes the fun out of it. Not knowing what everyone will turn up with is part of the evening.

A common middle ground is to add a rough budget when you hand out roles. Assign "main," "dessert," "drinks," and so on, and mention "around $30 each." That alone narrows the gap between the person who brings snacks and the person who orders the cake.

Some gap will remain, because different roles have different price ceilings. A whole cake often costs more than $30, and drinks swing with the headcount. If there's still a difference of tens of dollars after you've set a budget, pooling the receipts is more reliable.

## 🧾 Pool the receipts and split them again

Treat everything people brought as if the group had bought it together. Add up what each person spent, divide by the headcount, and settle the differences between those who paid more and those who paid less.

Here's a five-person potluck.

| Role | Spent | Fair share | Balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cake | $48 | $27 | Gets $21 |
| Chicken | $36 | $27 | Gets $9 |
| Drinks | $30 | $27 | Gets $3 |
| Salad (homemade) | $12 | $27 | Pays $15 |
| Snacks | $9 | $27 | Pays $18 |

The total is $135, or $27 each. The snack buyer sends the cake buyer $18, and the salad maker sends $3 to the cake buyer, $9 to the chicken buyer, and $3 to the drinks buyer. Now everyone has paid the same.

For homemade dishes, **record only the cost of ingredients**. Start pricing in labor and the best cooks end up with the biggest bills, and the potluck loses its easy feel. Thank the cook some other way, such as letting them skip the cleanup.

In Evere, record each item as its own expense with whoever bought it as the payer, make everyone a participant, and use an equal split. The settlement screen pulls the transfers together, so nobody has to work out who owes whom.

## 🏠 What the host is quietly paying for

Whoever hosts carries costs that aren't on any receipt. They tidy up, run the heating, put out the dishes, and clean afterward. Because none of it shows up on paper, it tends to fall out of the settlement.

There are a few ways to handle it. Let the host bring nothing. Or take the host out of the food split and have everyone else cover it. In the example above, removing the host from the split adds $6.75 to each of the other four. Which one you pick matters less than avoiding **a hosting burden that piles up on the same person every year without anyone saying a word**.

## 🎁 Keep the gift exchange out of the settlement

A gift exchange, whether it's Secret Santa or a white elephant, involves everyone buying their own gift, so there's nothing to settle. What it does need is **a single budget figure everyone agrees on**.

Without one, someone shows up with a $10 trinket and someone else with a $50 designer item, and the person who opens the cheap one feels awkward. Saying "around $20" with one number keeps prices close. Saying "up to $20" tends to split people into those who spend the minimum and those who go right to the limit.

A joint gift, where everyone chips in for one present for one person, is a different matter. For how to collect for that, see [Group Gifts: Collecting the Money Without Chasing People](/en/blog/group-gift-collection/).

## ⏰ Order the cake and the main on the day the headcount is final

Holiday cakes and party platters often need to be ordered early, and some shops take payment when you order. From that moment, whoever ordered is fronting the money for everyone.

Make the order deadline the same day the guest list is locked. If someone drops out after you've ordered, the amount of food stays the same while the number of people splitting it shrinks. Agree up front that **once food is ordered, the cost exists whether or not everyone comes**. For handling last-minute dropouts, see [Who Pays When Someone No-Shows a Group Booking?](/en/blog/cancellation-fee-who-pays/)

A potluck is a nice kind of gathering because money stays in the background. That's exactly why it helps to have one quiet way to even things out when the gap gets wide, so the same group can meet again happily next year.
