When two people decide to get an animal, the conversation is usually about breed and name. The money part ends at "we'll just split it".

That agreement holds fine for the first year. It breaks the day a bill arrives that nobody planned for, because pet costs come in three layers of very different character, and **only the third layer is what people fall out over**.

## 🐾 The costs arrive in three layers

The first is setup: the adoption or purchase, the crate and litter tray, the first round of vaccinations. Large, one-off, and known before the animal arrives. Almost nobody argues here.

The second is running costs: food, litter, insurance premiums, grooming. Roughly the same amount leaves every month, which means it can be handled exactly like any other household bill.

The third is unplanned veterinary care. The amount is unknowable, the timing isn't yours to choose, and it demands a decision on the spot. The reason a fifty-fifty agreement stops working here isn't the size of the bill. It's that **the decision to spend and the decision to split arrive in the same moment**. Agreeing to halve things settles how to divide a payment, not how to decide on one.

## 🧮 Look at running costs by the year

Viewed month by month, running costs never show you the real weight. Suppose the breakdown looks like this, laid out annually.

| Item | Frequency | Each | Per year |
|---|---|---|---|
| Food | Monthly | $60 | $720 |
| Litter and supplies | Monthly | $20 | $240 |
| Vaccinations and check-up | Yearly | $200 | $200 |
| Pet insurance | Monthly | $30 | $360 |

That totals 720 + 240 + 200 + 360, or $1,520 a year, which is $126.67 a month.

The value of the annual view is that it changes the insurance decision. Here the premium is $360 a year, close to a quarter of all running costs. If that reads as expensive, dropping it is a legitimate call, but making it means you've decided the two of you will carry the entire third layer yourselves. **Insurance is the mechanism that converts unplanned costs into running costs**, and it's worth naming that trade rather than backing into it.

## 🏥 Set a ceiling, not a percentage

"Halve it if it happens" isn't enough. What you actually need is a line: how much either of you can authorise alone.

Something like "up to $400, whoever is there decides and pays, above that we ring each other." With that line agreed, nobody stands in an emergency clinic at midnight wondering whether to wait for a call back. The number itself matters less than its existence.

Above that tier, surgery or long-term treatment, the question stops being financial and becomes a question of what you each believe is right for the animal. You don't have to resolve that in advance. You do want to have touched it once, because **two people discovering they hold opposite views for the first time in a waiting room** is the worst version of this conversation.

## 📄 Write down ownership and what happens if you split up

For a couple living together, this is the item everyone wants to skip. It also carries the highest price for skipping it.

Three decisions: whose name the registration and microchip sit under, who keeps the animal if the household separates, and whether the other person keeps contributing. Settling the third one now means there's no negotiation about money at the exact moment neither of you can negotiate well.

Put the registration under whoever is more likely to keep the animal, purely because it makes the paperwork simpler. Registration doesn't decide whose animal it is; the record of who paid for what does that, provided the record exists. For dividing household costs generally, see [the guide to splitting living costs](/en/blog/cohabitation-expense-split/), and for separating a shared home, [settling up when you stop living together](/en/blog/cohabitation-breakup-settlement/).

## 🔁 Keep it out of the monthly settlement

Fold running costs into groceries and utilities and you lose the ability to explain why a month moved. Buying food in bulk once makes it look as though your living costs jumped.

**Give pet costs their own line from the start.** [A separate project](/en/docs/projects/) in Evere keeps a running total apart from the household. Being able to see what the year actually cost is what makes next year's insurance and food decisions concrete rather than theoretical.

Putting the payments on one person's card keeps the record in one place, too. It's the same logic as a shared subscription: [fixing who pays and correcting it at settlement](/en/blog/shared-subscriptions/) takes less effort than deciding whose turn it is every time.

It looks like a lot to agree on, but the whole thing takes about half an hour, once, before the animal arrives. After that it's just adding lines for the next fifteen years.
