Eat out with Thai friends and the middle of the table fills up with dishes: tom yum, pad kra pao, stir-fried morning glory. Everyone takes a little of everything. Nobody can say who ate how much of what.

With a meal like that, paying only for your own order barely works. So it's natural that **Thai friends usually split evenly, หารเท่า (haan thao)**, given how the table is set.

## 🍲 Shared dishes, equal shares

A Thai meal is rarely one plate per person. The group orders together and eats together, so dividing the total by headcount is the simplest approach, and there's little reason to break it down further.

When you do want to pay only for yourself, say you just had a drink at a café, ต่างคนต่างจ่าย (taang khon taang jaai, each pays their own) gets the message across. You'll also often hear อเมริกันแชร์ (American share) used for splitting the bill.

## 🧓 Elders and seniors treat

Thailand also has a custom of older or more senior people treating younger ones. A senior colleague picking up lunch for the junior staff, or an elder paying at a family gathering, isn't unusual. Treating is เลี้ยง (liang), as in "วันนี้พี่เลี้ยงเอง" (today it's on me). พี่ means older sibling, and older people use it to refer to themselves.

**When you're treated, thank them and accept graciously.** Among friends your own age, even splits are the norm, so if being treated by someone older every time bothers you, treat them next time or return the favor another way.

## 🧾 Service charge and VAT on the bill

What surprises many visitors at Thai restaurants is the gap between menu prices and the bill. In restaurants inside hotels and shopping malls, a service charge of up to 10% and 7% VAT may be added to food prices. A "++" on the menu means both are charged on top.

Restaurants that collect a service charge are required to display it clearly. When splitting, **divide the final total including service charge and tax, not the sum of menu prices**. Street stalls and casual eateries usually don't add these charges, so the menu price is what you pay.

Some people leave small change as a tip, but where a service charge is included, it isn't expected. For whether to include tips in a split at all, see [Who Pays the Tip? Splitting It, or Keeping It Personal](/en/blog/tipping-and-splitting/).

## 📱 PromptPay and collecting through LINE

What makes splitting easy in Thailand is PromptPay. Using a phone number or a QR code, people send money instantly from their banking apps. A common pattern is for the person who paid to show their QR code on their phone and everyone else to scan it and pay.

After paying, it's standard to post the transfer confirmation screen, the "slip," in the LINE group to show you've paid. Kasikornbank has offered a chatbot called ขุนทอง (Khun Thong) that you invite into a LINE group to create a bill and even chase people who haven't paid. **Collecting and chasing payments without leaving LINE** is a distinctive part of how Thais split bills.

**PromptPay requires a Thai bank account**, though, so visitors usually can't use it. When eating with Thai friends, handing over your share in cash is the quickest option.

If you're traveling in Thailand with friends from home, it's usually easiest for one person to pay by card and settle up in your own currency afterward.
