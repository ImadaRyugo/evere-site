The contents of the fridge become a question in the first month of living together, without fail. Ingredients for dinners you cook together sit on the same shelf as things only one of you touches.

Agreeing to split food costs down the middle doesn't settle this, because **agreeing to split doesn't specify what's being split**. So a small judgement call happens on every shopping trip, and one of you quietly absorbs the result.

## 🛒 Where the grey area actually forms

Dinner you cook together is obvious. The protein powder only one of you drinks is obvious. It's everything in between that's the problem.

Bread and coffee for breakfast, where one of you eats it daily and the other twice a week. Milk that goes into cooking and into cereal. Ice cream in the freezer, where the person buying it isn't the person finishing it.

Things like this make up twenty to thirty per cent of a typical receipt. In money that's somewhere between $100 and $200 a month, which **compounds into something you can't ignore over a year**. Nobody raises it because each individual instance is worth about three dollars.

## 🧴 Household supplies: if it lives in the flat, it's shared

Toiletries and cleaning goods are easier to judge than food. Toilet paper, detergent, bin bags, tissues, cleaning products. They sit in the flat, both of you use them, and treating them as shared causes no trouble.

Personal care is where it divides. If you use the same shampoo and body wash, they're shared; if you each buy your own, they're personal. That's determined by what's actually happening in the bathroom, so it doesn't need a discussion.

The awkward category is things only one of you gets through in volume. If one person baths daily, bath products lean personal. If one person wears makeup, remover is personal. As a rule, anything with an obviously lopsided consumption rate belongs on one side.

## 🍺 If only one of you consumes it, it's theirs

Alcohol, cigarettes, supplements, a specific brand of hot sauce, the expensive yoghurt one of you insists on. Anything consumed by one person is cleanest as a personal cost regardless of the amount.

Put it in the shared pot and the non-drinker is funding the wine. At small amounts nobody minds; somewhere past $100 a month the atmosphere changes. Keeping it personal also frees the person buying it from having to think about how much they're buying, which they'll appreciate.

When you can't decide, ask **whether this item would exist in the flat if that person didn't live here**. If it wouldn't, it's personal.

## 📏 Pick exactly one rule

Decide each of the above cases individually and you end up with twenty rules that neither of you remembers. What actually runs is a single rule.

The one worth recommending: if both of you eat it or both of you use it, it's shared. Differences in frequency and volume are ignored. Someone eating the bread twice a week still counts as eating the bread. That coarseness is what drops the cost of deciding to nearly zero.

The other workable rule is a money threshold. Anything personal over about $20 in a single shop is personal, everything below is shared. You stop counting small items entirely, which removes the receipt-sorting work. The underlying split models are compared in [three ways to divide living costs](/en/blog/cohabitation-expense-split/).

## 🧮 A month of shopping, split out

A household spending $720 a month on food and supplies. Clearly shared items come to $480, things only Alex consumes come to $144, and things only Bo consumes come to $96.

| | Half of shared | Personal | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alex | $240 | $144 | $384 |
| Bo | $240 | $96 | $336 |

That's $720. The gap between them is $48, which is precisely the gap in their personal consumption.

Split the whole thing evenly instead and they'd each pay $360. Alex would be $24 better off and Bo $24 worse off. Small in a month; $576 over a year. **Looking at that figure and deciding it doesn't matter is a completely different act from never looking.**

Recurring costs are covered in [managing shared subscriptions](/en/blog/shared-subscriptions/). In Evere, log shared shopping as an [even split](/en/docs/expenses/) and personal items as expenses for that person alone, and the table above appears at the end of the month.

## ❓ Common questions

**Isn't separating receipts every time exhausting?**
You don't separate them. Rather than asking the cashier to ring things up separately, just enter the personal items as their own line when you record it. Remembering only the larger personal items after the fact is coarse enough to work.

**What if I buy something that's only for my partner?**
Record it as you fronting the cost, with them as the person it's for. If things you were asked to pick up become shared costs, the person doing the shopping loses money on every trip, and shortly afterwards nobody volunteers to go.

**With different incomes, do we apply the ratio to personal items too?**
No, keep personal items personal. The ratio applies to shared costs only. Once one person is funding the other's personal consumption, you start having opinions about how much they consume, which is the opposite of what the ratio was for.

**Are meals out a shared cost?**
Shared when you go together, personal when one of you goes with friends. Some people worry about the groceries at home going to waste, but chasing that far kills the whole system. Judge it on whether both of you ate.
