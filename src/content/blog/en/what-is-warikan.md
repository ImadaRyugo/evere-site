To go Dutch is to share the cost of a meal or an outing instead of letting one person pay. It's an old, informal phrase, and most English speakers understand it instantly.

What fewer people notice is that it covers two different arrangements. And that small ambiguity is behind a surprising number of awkward moments at the end of a meal.

## Two meanings in one phrase

Wiktionary defines go Dutch as to "pay for one's own food and bills, or split the cost, when eating at a restaurant or going out for entertainment."

The first meaning is that **each person pays for what they ordered**. The second is that the total is divided evenly. Its parent phrase, Dutch treat, leans toward the first: an occasion where everyone pays for their own expenses.

In practice, people use go Dutch for both. That's fine when everyone ordered roughly the same. But when one person had a steak and another had a side salad, "let's go Dutch" can mean $45 to one person and $28 to the other. If prices vary, **say which one you mean**: "everyone pays for their own" or "let's split it evenly." For when each approach works better, see [Split the Bill or Separate Checks? Which Is Actually Faster](/en/blog/split-vs-separate-checks/).

## Where the Dutch comes from

The Dutch in go Dutch isn't a compliment. English has a long list of phrases that use Dutch to mean something lesser or fake: Dutch courage, Dutch comfort, a Dutch bargain. The Oxford English Dictionary, as cited on Wikipedia, ties these to the rivalry and hostility between England and the Netherlands in the 17th century.

A Dutch treat fits the pattern: **a "treat" in which nobody actually treats anybody**. Wiktionary dates go Dutch, the verb form, to 1914.

Most people who say it today don't mean any insult; the phrase lost its edge long ago. It even traveled abroad: in Korea, the everyday word for splitting the bill is 더치페이, a borrowing of "Dutch pay," a phrase English speakers themselves don't use.

## Phrases that sit close to it

English has several ways to talk about sharing a bill. The differences come down to who is splitting, and on what basis.

| Phrase | Meaning | How it differs from going Dutch |
|---|---|---|
| Split the bill | Divide the total between people | The most neutral option; usually means equal shares |
| Go halves | Split something 50/50 | Two people; common in the UK and Australia |
| Separate checks | Each person gets their own bill | Paying your own, settled by the restaurant |
| Chip in | Contribute some money | Often unequal amounts toward a shared cost |
| My treat / It's on me | One person pays for everyone | The opposite of going Dutch |
| Pay your share | Cover the part assigned to you | Describes the result, however it was divided |

When a couple says they split the rent, they usually mean go halves. With two people, dividing evenly and dividing in half are the same thing.

Equal splits have a known downside: the light eater or the non-drinker pays for food and drinks they didn't have. Some groups adjust for it up front. Ways to do that are in [Should Non-Drinkers Pay the Same Share of the Bill?](/en/blog/non-drinkers-bill-split/)

## Three decisions hidden in "let's split it"

The phrase is short, but any real split depends on three decisions: which costs are being split, who is splitting them, and in what proportions.

At a single dinner, all three answer themselves. On a trip, or with a household budget, they don't. ## Common questions

**Q. Is it rude to say go Dutch?**
Not in everyday use. The phrase comes from old jabs at the Dutch, but it's been used neutrally for generations. If you'd rather avoid it, "split the bill" means the same thing without the history.

**Q. Does going Dutch mean equal shares or paying for your own?**
It can mean either. If the prices of what people ordered are close, it doesn't matter. If they aren't, say which you mean before the bill arrives.

**Q. Is go Dutch used on dates?**
Yes. It's one of the most common contexts for the phrase. Whether a date should go Dutch is a matter of the couple's preferences, and saying it plainly beats guessing.
