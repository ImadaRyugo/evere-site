Eat out with Korean friends and the end of the meal can be confusing. With people your own age, the bill gets split without a second thought. With someone a few years older, they may have paid before you've even reached for your wallet. Which one is normal in Korea? **Both are.**

Splitting the bill has become firmly established in Korea, while the custom of seniors treating juniors is still very much alive. Knowing how people move between the two saves you a lot of hesitation at the counter.

## 🍚 Deochi-pei and N-ppang have taken hold

The Korean word for splitting the bill is 더치페이 (deochi-pei), and if you want to be clear about equal shares, N빵 (en-ppang). The differences between the words are covered in [How to Say Split the Bill in Japanese, Korean, Chinese, Thai](/en/blog/split-the-bill-phrases/).

What stands out even more than the vocabulary is how fast people settle up. Among friends, one person often pays by card at the counter, and everyone else has sent their share by phone before they're out the door. That ease comes from the payment apps' split features, which we'll get to below.

## 🧑‍🤝‍🧑 Seniors still treat

At the same time, the habit of senior students or colleagues buying meals for juniors runs deep. A well-known pattern is that **the person who was treated returns a smaller favor**: if your senior pays for dinner, you get the coffee afterward.

At 회식 (hoesik), the after-work team dinner, the boss or the company often covers the cost, and colleagues rarely split it among themselves.

When the group moves on to a second spot after dinner, people sometimes take turns by venue: "I got the first round, you get this one." One person pays per place, so there's no math on the spot, but the amounts differ. Anyone who minds can adjust the difference later.

When an older person treats you, saying "잘 먹었습니다" (jal meogeosseumnida, thank you for the meal) is basic courtesy. If they keep treating you, tell them up front that next time is on you, so the relationship doesn't become one-sided.

## 💑 Couples, equal shares, and date accounts

On dating costs, too, support for equal shares stands out among younger Koreans. In a survey of 3,729 people aged 14 to 18 published by Woori Bank in 2025, **83.9% of girls said dating costs should be split exactly in half**, compared with 50.6% of boys ([as reported by SBS Biz](https://biz.sbs.co.kr/amp/article/20000214439)).

A familiar idea among Korean couples is the 데이트 통장 (date account). Both partners put a set amount into a shared account every month and pay for dates from it. No splitting at every bill, and the balance shows when you're overspending. In the same survey, 44.3% of boys and 34.2% of girls were positive about opening one.

It's close to a shared household account, except that it's limited to dates.

## 📱 Split features in payment apps

What keeps splitting so easy in Korea is the split feature in payment apps. KakaoPay's 정산하기 (settle up), which works inside KakaoTalk chats, divides a total evenly among the members of a chat and sends each of them a payment request. It even has a ladder game (사다리타기) that assigns amounts at random, which is a very Korean touch.

Other payment and banking apps offer similar features. Most of them assume a Korean phone number or bank account, though, which makes them hard for visitors to use.

## ✈️ Eating out with Korean friends

Whether you're visiting Korea or hosting Korean friends at home, the moments to watch are where the two customs meet.

If someone older tries to treat you, there's no need to insist on splitting. Accept graciously and say the next one is on you. Among friends your own age, on the other hand, nobody will mind if you suggest N-ppang.

If you pay in won on a trip and settle up in your own currency at home, you'll need to agree on which exchange rate to use. ## ❓ Common questions

**Q. Do restaurants in Korea split the bill for you?**
It depends on the place. Some will split it at the counter, but the usual approach is for one person to pay and the others to transfer their share. Assume one payment per table and you won't be caught out.

**Q. With a Korean partner, is it better to split or to treat?**
It depends on the two of you. In the teen survey above, most supported splitting in half, but the view that the older partner or the man should pay more is still around. Talking about it once early on prevents misunderstandings.

**Q. If a senior treats me, do I need to pay them back?**
Not with an equal amount. Buying coffee or getting the next meal is the usual way to return the favor.
