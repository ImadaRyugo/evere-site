When a club buys something together, the argument almost never happens at the moment of purchase. It happens six months later, when one of the buyers announces they're leaving.

"What happens to the money I put in?" is a question most clubs cannot answer on the spot. **Decide it before you buy, or relitigate it every time somebody goes.**

## Kit and equipment are two different purchases

Start by separating where the things end up.

Shirts, boots and rackets go home with an individual. Nets, ball carts, tents and first aid kits stay with the club. Those two sit on the same order form and behave completely differently in the accounts.

The first is not really a joint purchase at all — it's a bulk discount. The second is joint ownership. Treating them as one thing is why the question has no answer when somebody leaves.

## What goes home belongs to whoever paid for it

A shirt belongs to the person who paid for it. They don't return it when they leave, and the club has no obligation to buy it back.

The only thing worth agreeing in advance is what happens to the discount. If twenty shirts bring the unit price down, that saving is spread evenly. If only ten people order and the discount doesn't apply, do those ten pay more, or does club money make up the difference? Settle that and the rest is ordinary shopping.

Extras like names, numbers and alterations go to whoever asked for them. Framing it as a joint purchase makes it tempting to split everything, but **splitting the cost of things people take home means the people who don't take one are paying for those who do.**

## What stays with the club is measured in years

The equipment is the real question. Twenty-four members buying $1,200 of kit is $50 each. Treat that $50 as an investment and every departure starts a refund conversation.

To stop that, fix a service life at the point of purchase. Assume five years and the annual cost is $240, or $10 per member per year. Nobody paid $50; everybody prepaid five years.

| | Amount |
|---|---|
| Equipment total | $1,200 |
| Members | 24 |
| Each | $50 |
| Service life | 5 years |
| Per member per year | $10 |

This is the same move as [treating camping gear as depreciation](/en/blog/camping-gear-fair-split/): the cost of a shared thing divides most defensibly across the years you own it. For longer-lived assets, this calculation sits one step before [separating fixed costs from usage costs](/en/blog/family-shared-assets-upkeep/).

## Charge the joiners instead of refunding the leavers

Somebody who leaves after one year has four years of value unused. Refund it and the club's cash falls while the equipment is still sitting in the store cupboard.

There is a way to keep the fairness without spending the cash. Instead of paying leavers out, charge people coming in.

Two years on, the remaining value is three-fifths of $1,200, or $720. Across 24 members that's $30. Anyone joining that year pays $30 as a joining fee, buying into what the existing members have already funded.

Refunds and joining fees correct the same unfairness, but **the cash flows in opposite directions.** A refund takes money out of the club; a joining fee brings money in. For a club that intends to carry on, the second one actually works. Present it alongside [how you set the dues](/en/blog/monthly-dues-management/), as a one-off paid on joining.

## Collect before you order

Finally, the simplest thing that helps: take the money before placing the order.

Bulk purchases are large. One person paying by card is carrying a four-figure sum for several weeks, and if anyone changes their mind after delivery, that person is left holding stock.

Collecting first prevents both. Set an order deadline and order only for the people who have paid. [Create a project](/en/docs/projects/) in Evere, mark people settled as they pay, and you know exactly who is outstanding before the order goes in.

## Common questions

**Q. What service life should we use?**
Slightly shorter than you expect the thing to last. A long life makes the annual figure look kind and leaves you short of money at replacement time.

**Q. What if the equipment breaks early?**
The residual value drops to zero, so the joining fee does too. Divide the replacement among whoever is a member at that point.

**Q. What about equipment handed back by leavers?**
Anything returned becomes a club asset, and if it still has value, add it to the residual. Don't strain to put a price on something visibly worn out; it ends the conversation faster.

**Q. A new member refuses to pay the joining fee.**
Waive it for that one person if you like, but record the waiver. Without the precedent written down, the rule disappears the next time it comes up.
