When friends hand you the wedding after-party, the frightening part isn't running the evening. It's **the ticket money not covering the bill, and the difference landing on your own card**.

What makes it worse is that this shortfall doesn't come from a mistake. It comes from pricing tickets against an assumed headcount, and the headcount moving on the night. Here's how to take that risk apart.

## 🥂 The loss comes from fixed costs, not from numbers

After-party spending splits into two kinds: what grows with each guest, and what costs the same whoever turns up.

Food and drink are the first kind. Fewer guests, smaller bill. Venue hire, prizes, flowers, and the AV or projector fee are the second kind, and they cost identically at thirty guests or forty.

Losses are born in that second group. Price the ticket as total spend divided by expected guests and you've quietly divided the fixed costs by headcount too. Every person who drops out takes their slice of the fixed cost with them, and that slice becomes a hole.

## 🧮 Work out the break-even headcount first

Take an evening with $1,300 of fixed costs, $35 per head for food and drink, and a $70 ticket.

| Guests | Ticket income | Spend | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| 40 | $2,800 | $2,700 | +$100 |
| 35 | $2,450 | $2,525 | -$75 |
| 30 | $2,100 | $2,350 | -$250 |

Spend is 1,300 + 35 × guests, so $2,700 at forty, $2,525 at thirty-five and $2,350 at thirty.

Of the $70 ticket, $35 disappears into that guest's own food and drink, leaving $35 towards the fixed costs. Since 1,300 ÷ 35 = 37.1, **the evening goes into the red the moment attendance drops below thirty-eight**. An event you planned for forty was three cancellations away from a loss.

Running that one line before you set the price shortens the whole discussion, because you can say out loud how far the numbers can fall before anyone is out of pocket.

## 🎫 Divide by 80% of what you expect

The fix is simple: divide the fixed costs by fewer people than you expect. Eighty per cent is a sound default.

Expecting forty, divide $1,300 by thirty-two. That's $40.63, plus the $35 variable cost per head, giving $75.63. Round it to $75 and that's your ticket. Break-even is now 1,300 ÷ 40 = 32.5, so thirty-three guests, and you can lose seven people without trouble.

Don't price to the nearest dollar. Change at the door eats your volunteers' time when you need them most. Round to the nearest five, and **always round in the direction that leaves a surplus**.

You don't have to refund a surplus either. Refunds create as much work as there are guests. Add another prize, print the photos, or put it towards the couple's gift, and spend it on the night. If a group present is running separately, keep [that collection](/en/blog/group-gift-collection/) on its own books so the two don't blend.

## 🚫 No-shows, walk-ins, and what you absorb

The last thing people argue about is what happens when the numbers move on the day. All three cases can be settled in the invitation.

Anchor no-shows to the date you confirm numbers with the venue. Write "we confirm numbers at 6pm Thursday, and cancellations after that are still charged." Whether that line exists changes how heavy the messages on the day feel. The reasoning and the wording are in [who pays when someone no-shows](/en/blog/cancellation-fee-who-pays/).

Walk-ins can generally be waved in. Once the fixed costs are covered, every extra guest adds the gap between ticket and variable cost. Some venues can't add covers at short notice, so say at the door that food may not stretch, and leave it there.

Decide your own ticket before you start, and tell everyone. Some parties comp the organisers, some have them pay like everyone else. Either works, but leaving it open means deciding it while you're counting cash at midnight. For the wider running order, [the organiser's guide to settling up](/en/blog/drinking-party-organizer-guide/) covers it.

## ❓ Common questions

**Q. Can the couple be asked to contribute?**
At a ticketed after-party they're guests, so as a rule they pay nothing. If they ask for grander prizes or flowers, having them cover only that difference is defensible. Record who covered what either way.

**Q. How many people do I need on the door?**
Two for forty guests. One takes the money, one checks names. Combining the roles creates a queue and pushes your start time back. Bring more small notes than you think you need and the line keeps moving.

**Q. Is it fine to put the venue bill on my own card first?**
Yes, if the amount and the collection are both predictable. Watch out when the venue invoices you days later, because you'll be carrying everyone's cash in the meantime. For that window, tell a co-organiser how much there is and where it's kept.

**Q. Should I publish the accounts afterwards?**
Only if there's a surplus, and then one line on where it went is enough. Publishing every line item invites comment on each of them and stretches the conversation for days. Knowing where the money went is all transparency requires here.
