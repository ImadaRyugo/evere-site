Talking about money with a partner isn't hard because of the content. It's hard because **the first sentence has never been written down**.

You already know what you want to say. Split it evenly, weight it towards whoever earns more, decide how the fronted money comes back. What's missing is a way to say it out loud, so it gets swallowed at the counter one more time. This is that first sentence, and the fifteen minutes that follow it.

## Have it somewhere other than home

At home the conversation runs long. There's nowhere to go, so your partner braces, and when they fall silent you have nothing to fill the gap with.

Do it outside. A café, or a walk. Somewhere with a natural time limit tightens the whole thing. There's a mild effect from being in public, but the bigger one is that **the end time exists before you start**. A conversation that everyone knows will take fifteen minutes is a much easier one to open.

Avoid the evening it went wrong. Raising the system right after an awkward bill turns a structural question into a comment on tonight, and all your partner will hear is an accusation. Do it on a day when nothing happened.

## Open with a report, not a question

"So, about money, what do you think?" makes people brace, because the conversation starts without them knowing what's coming.

Report your own state instead. Ask for no verdict, put down only a fact. That removes them from the position of being evaluated, so they don't need to defend anything.

> I've lost track of who's paid for what lately and it's bugging me a bit. Can we sort it out once, properly?

Nothing in that sentence blames anyone. The one with the problem is you, and the only thing being requested is permission to tidy up. Once you have the yes, the rest is admin.

Sending it as a message first works well too. It gives them time to think instead of starting cold across a table. Even a one-word reply means the conversation is now scheduled.

## Lines you can use as they are

Once you're in, propose rather than consult. Asking "what do you reckon?" invites politeness, and politeness produces no opinions.

> I think we're mostly fine as we are, I just want to treat rent and food separately from everything else.

> How about we halve the big things and whoever pays keeps the small stuff?

If the gap in what you each earn is the real issue, saying the number is faster than circling it.

> We earn pretty differently, so I'd rather do something like 70/30. An exact half means one of us is always stretching.

When it's about money you've fronted, take the chasing tone out of it.

> Can we do the ones we cover for each other as one settle-up at the end of the month? Doing it each time is awkward and I'll forget anyway.

## What to say when they go quiet

Silence isn't refusal. Usually it means they don't know what the right answer is supposed to be.

Filling the silence with more explanation makes your case longer and leaves them with even less room to answer. Rebuild the question into something easy to answer instead.

> You don't have to decide now. Just tell me which one you'd hate.

> Straight down the middle, or proportional. Which feels less annoying?

If the answer is "whatever you want", don't push. **"Whatever you want" usually means "I haven't thought about it yet"**, so leave one proposal on the table and check back another day.

> Let's try it this way for a month, and tell me if it stops working.

## Decide three things, not everything

Trying to settle it all in one conversation guarantees you won't finish. Keep it to three.

What counts as shared. In what proportion it gets split. When you settle up. With those three answered, the day-to-day decisions make themselves. Larger items like a pet or a holiday can wait for their own conversation.

Once you've agreed, don't leave it as spoken words. Keeping the amounts somewhere means the next conversation doesn't start with "what did we say again?". ## Common questions

**Q. My partner hates talking about money. Now what?**
Tell them upfront that it happens once. What they dislike is rarely the amounts, it's the open-ended discussion. Naming a limit, "fifteen minutes and we're done", raises the odds of a yes considerably.

**Q. I earn more. How should I open?**
Lead with the conclusion that you'll pay more. Starting from ratios puts them in the position of justifying their income. Saying "I'd like to cover a bigger share" first, then discussing the split, keeps the conversation between equals.

**Q. Do we need this before living together?**
Not while your spending levels roughly match. It becomes necessary when larger amounts start moving, like a trip or a shared appliance. Having the conversation once before the amounts grow makes everything after it easier.

**Q. We talked, and a fortnight later we're back to how it was.**
Agreements that aren't written down last about two weeks. Paper or a notes app is fine, as long as it's somewhere you can both see. What fails isn't willpower, it's the absence of anything to remind you.
