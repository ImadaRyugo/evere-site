Moving in with parents changes the shape of a household budget. One house, one electricity meter, one fridge — and two households with different incomes and different daily rhythms living off them.

Leave that unaddressed and usually **both sides spend years quietly believing they are the ones putting in more.** The amount isn't the problem. Nobody can see what is actually being shared.

## Two households, one wallet

For the first few months everyone avoids making it awkward. Whoever goes to the shop pays, the electricity comes out of the parents' account, the rice comes from their cupboard.

That is comfortable and it doesn't last. **The total is invisible while everyone is mildly preoccupied with who is contributing more**, which is the most tiring arrangement available.

The first job isn't deciding the split. It's defining the shared pot: what belongs to the house (utilities, broadband, property costs, repairs) and what belongs to a household (most food, toiletries, insurance, phones). Once that line exists, the ratios can be adjusted later.

## Cut fixed costs into standing charge and usage

Utilities are where the conversation stalls. By headcount? By floor area? By hours spent at home? Each has a case.

The version people accept most readily splits the bill in two. The standing charge exists because the house exists, so the two households halve it. Only the usage portion gets divided by people.

Two parents, three in the younger household, $320 a month for electricity and gas, of which $80 is standing charge.

| Item | Amount | Parents | Younger household |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standing charge (halved) | $80 | $40 | $40 |
| Usage (2:3) | $240 | $96 | $144 |
| Total | $320 | $136 | $184 |

$136 plus $184 is $320. If you also want to reflect who is home all day, the method in [splitting utilities when one of you works from home](/en/blog/utilities-remote-work/) applies directly: change the ratio, keep the structure.

## Food follows the number of meals eaten together

Food is the vaguest part of living together, because rice and condiments are shared, breakfast isn't, and dinner is joint maybe half the week.

Make all of it shared and the household that eats separately subsidises the other. Separate all of it and nobody knows what to do about the fridge.

Two tiers works. Staples and cleaning supplies go in the shared pot. Actual meals get counted by how many were eaten together. Once the weekly pattern settles, switch to a flat monthly figure. The trigger for switching is the one in [the grocery grey area](/en/blog/groceries-gray-area-split/): when the per-meal arithmetic becomes tedious, it has earned a flat rate.

## Assume the two sides can't carry the same load

A household living on a pension and a household drawing salaries feel the same number differently. That is why an even split reads as unfair: it ignores the difference.

Deciding the earners carry everything has its own cost. The side that puts in no money finds it harder to have a say in the house, which is not a healthy shape for the arrangement.

Converting amounts into ratios works the way [proportional splitting by income](/en/blog/income-based-split-couples/) describes. Living together adds one more option: if the parents own the house, treat that as a contribution in kind. Book a notional rent and reduce their cash share by it. If you do that, **agree what the notional rent is at the start, and write it down.**

## Common questions

**Q. We're told that keeping records is cold.**
A record isn't only for billing. Once a year it lets both households see what the house actually costs. Looking at the number and deciding to leave things exactly as they are is also an agreement.

**Q. The parents refuse to charge rent.**
No need to force it, but check that utilities and repairs haven't quietly become theirs too. No rent and no running costs are different propositions.

**Q. Does this split change if care becomes necessary?**
Yes. Medical and care costs behave differently from living costs, so handle them as a separate pot. Where there are siblings, [splitting a parent's care costs](/en/blog/sibling-elder-care-costs/) covers the method.

**Q. How do we divide the cost of rebuilding or extending the house?**
Keep it away from living costs and settle ownership shares alongside the money. Money put in without that being explicit turns into an inheritance problem every time.
