A $120 bill, a $20 coupon, $100 actually paid.

Four ways, that's $25 each. Easy so far. It gets harder when one person produced the coupon, or when only one dish was half price. The question left standing is **whose saving the $20 actually is**.

## Discounts come in three kinds, with three answers

This looks complicated because one word covers several different things. Separated out, each has a clear answer.

| Kind of discount | Example | Whose saving |
|---|---|---|
| On the whole bill | 10% off the total, a visit offer | Everyone, diluted proportionally |
| On a specific item | One free drink, half price on a dish | Whoever ordered it |
| Brought by one person | A membership coupon, a birthday offer | Their call |

When it isn't obvious, look at what the discount was applied to rather than who made it possible. Applied to the bill, everyone; applied to an item, whoever ordered that item. That split is the easiest one to explain out loud.

## A discount on the whole bill dilutes itself

The kind taken off the total distributes fairly without anyone doing anything.

When $120 becomes $100, the same ratio lands on everybody's order. Someone who ordered $30 worth pays $25; someone who ordered $60 worth pays $50. Splitting equally works out the same way — divide the discounted total by heads and everyone benefits identically.

Which means for this kind, **looking only at the amount actually paid requires no thought at all**. There's no step where you divide the pre-discount figure and then redistribute the saving.

## A discount on one item belongs to whoever ordered it

Targeted discounts behave differently: a free drink, money off a lunch set, half price on particular dishes.

Share those across everyone and the people who didn't order the item gain while the person who did gains nothing. Reducing the bill of whoever ordered the discounted item is what matches reality.

Calculating that strictly makes splitting heavy, though. The rough test is whether the discount comes to more than a couple of dollars a head. Below that, treat it as a discount on the whole bill; above it, take it off the relevant person. Evere's [fixed-amount split mode](/en/docs/expenses/) lets you record exactly that, lowering one person's share.

The underlying practice of tying dishes to the people who ordered them is laid out in [equal, ratio or manual: choosing a split mode](/en/blog/three-split-methods/).

## A coupon somebody brought is theirs to direct

This is the one where opinions genuinely divide: one person had a coupon or a membership perk, and everybody's bill came down.

Two positions exist. Share it out, so everyone gains a little, or credit it to the person who brought it, so only their share falls.

The version that causes least trouble in practice is **letting the person who brought it decide**. Some will say it's their coupon and take it off their own share. Others will say it was about to expire anyway, so put it across the table. Both are legitimate. The moment a third party rules on what "should" happen, nobody produces a coupon again.

It's structurally the same argument as the organiser putting everything on their own card and keeping the points. Who owns a perk somebody brought is covered in [is it unfair for the organiser to keep the points](/en/blog/organizer-points-debate/).

## Record the amount actually paid

Finally, the record. Whichever treatment you picked, what goes in is the money that actually left the account.

$100, not $120. Obvious in principle, easy to get wrong in practice, because the total at the top of the receipt is the pre-discount one. **Record the pre-discount figure and everyone's share comes out higher than what they owe.**

If you want the discount itself documented, put it in a note or attach a photo of the receipt. There's no need to restore the original amount. Tracking down why a total doesn't reconcile is covered in [when the receipt doesn't add up to the amount charged](/en/blog/receipt-mismatch/).

## Common questions

**Q. One person signed up for a membership to get the discount.**
Treat it as theirs to direct. If they say to share it out, follow that. The decision sits with whoever brought it.

**Q. Part of the bill was paid with points.**
No cash left for that part, so deduct it from the recorded amount. Whose points they were is handled exactly like a coupon.

**Q. The discount was conditional on the number of people.**
Ten per cent off for four or more exists because everybody was there. Treat it as a discount on the whole bill without hesitating.

**Q. We were given a voucher for next time.**
It has nothing to do with this bill, so it stays out of the record. Just decide who takes it home, and using it later won't cause an argument.
