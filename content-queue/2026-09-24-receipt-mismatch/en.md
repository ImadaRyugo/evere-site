You settle up, look at the receipt, and the items listed don't add up to what left your account. That's the moment the split stalls.

A few cents you can round away. A few dollars you can't. And because the figure is wrong before it gets divided, leaving it alone makes everyone's share wrong.

## 🔍 Look at the difference itself first

The first move isn't checking the items one by one. It's **working out the gap and looking at the shape of that number**.

Exactly ten per cent of the total is a service charge. A per-head multiple is a cover charge or table fee. A round number is a discount or a keying error. The gap usually identifies itself.

| Shape of the gap | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| Around 10% of the total | Service charge |
| Matches the local tax rate | Tax shown differently |
| Divides evenly by headcount | Cover charge or table fee |
| Equals one item's price | Double-counted or mistyped |

Read it this way and most gaps resolve in under a minute. Adding items from the top down takes longer and goes wrong more often.

## 🍽️ What gets added after the item list

In restaurants the usual culprit is everything that isn't food.

Service charge is typically around ten per cent of the total and appears at the foot of the receipt. Cover charges and table fees are per head. Some places add a late-night rate.

These share one property: **they never appear in the item list but are definitely in the amount charged**. Add up the top half of the receipt and you will always be short.

For dividing, a service charge is proportional to the total, so it apportions itself in line with what each person consumed. A per-head cover charge divides by heads instead. How to handle that alongside people who didn't drink is in [the organiser's guide to splitting a group dinner](/en/blog/drinking-party-organizer-guide/).

## 🧾 Tax shown one way on the menu, another on the receipt

Menu prices excluding tax with a receipt including it is still a common combination.

When that happens the figure you carried in your head and the charge will always differ, by exactly the tax rate, which makes it easy to spot.

The awkward version is **a single receipt containing items at different tax rates**. Takeaway alongside eat-in puts two rates on one bill. Divide using the tax-inclusive total only. Start chasing rates item by item and the effort outgrows the point of splitting at all.

## 📄 The bill was split across more than one docket

At larger tables the bill itself sometimes runs to several dockets. Some places open a new one for later rounds, or split when a group moves table.

When that happens, the receipt in your hand is only part of the story. Nothing fails to add up — **you're holding an incomplete set**. Asking "is that everything?" at the till is the only real prevention.

The same shape appears when one person has already paid their own way. Check whether their share has been taken out of what remains. When to use separate checks at all is covered in [splitting the bill versus separate checks](/en/blog/split-vs-separate-checks/).

A card statement differing from the receipt is a separate matter. The reasoning, including overseas use, is in [keeping records when cash, card and QR payments mix abroad](/en/blog/mixed-payment-methods-abroad/).

## ⚖️ What to do with a gap you can't explain

Some gaps survive investigation. Deciding in advance how to treat them keeps you from standing at the till working it out.

The workable rule is to **treat the amount charged as authoritative and divide that by heads**. What actually left the account is the fact; the itemisation is commentary. The breakdown failing to reconcile and the payment being final are two separate things.

That final figure is also what goes into Evere. [Log the expense](/en/docs/expenses/) with the amount actually charged and the division stays correct whether or not the mystery is ever solved. Attach a photo of the receipt and you can come back to it later.

Only question it with the restaurant when the gap is large and genuinely doesn't sit right. **What can be fixed by asking at the counter stops being fixable the next day.** The window to check closes before the question of who was right ever gets settled.
