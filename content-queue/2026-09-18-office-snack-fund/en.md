Office coffee and snacks almost always start with one person buying them. And then that person keeps buying them.

The amounts are small enough that nobody calls it a problem. Only the person buying knows what it comes to each month, and months go by without them finding a way to say so.

## 🍪 The tipping point isn't frequency, it's headcount

Collecting per round works while the group is small. With three of you, taking a dollar each from whoever is there finishes the job.

It breaks when the group grows. **Past five people, somebody is always absent.** The one who stepped out when you got back, the one working from home that day, the one joining next week. The moment you have to decide who to ask and who to skip, per-round collection stops being maintainable.

Frequency is the second trigger. Weekly, you can track it as you go. Daily, the chasing costs more than the money. Past five people, or past twice a week, it's time for a pot.

## 💰 Set the contribution backwards from real costs

The usual mistake is picking a round number first. Decide on five dollars a head and then work out what it buys, and you end up either short or sitting on a surplus.

**Do it the other way around: total a month of actual spending, then divide by the headcount.**

| Item | Monthly actual |
|---|---|
| Coffee beans and filters | $20 |
| Tea | $8 |
| Shared snacks | $30 |
| Total | $58 |

Ten people makes it $5.80 each. Rounding that to $6 is fine, but **decide in advance whose the rounding is**. Does it bank, or go towards next month's shopping? Left vague and repeated, there will come a moment when it looks like the organiser is skimming.

## 👀 A pot without a visible balance dies

Pots don't collapse because people resent the amount. **They collapse because nobody can say how much is currently in it.**

From the contributor's side, paying every month with no idea where it went feels wrong regardless of size. And the organiser starts feeling vaguely guilty about not having an answer ready.

What's needed isn't proper accounting. The balance and the last few purchases will do. [Set up one project for the pot](/en/docs/projects/) in Evere and what came in sits next to what went out, so answering the question is a matter of turning the screen round.

## 🙅 What to do about the people who don't drink it

There will always be someone who doesn't drink coffee or doesn't eat the snacks. Charge everyone the same and those people quietly subsidise the rest.

Three ways to handle it:

- Let each person choose whether they're in
- Split by item, with separate pots for coffee and tea
- Offer a reduced tier

The first causes the least trouble in practice. Splitting by item doubles the admin, and tiered amounts mean remembering who sits where. **Keeping it to in or out is the version that stays light enough to survive.**

The same question — whether people who don't partake should pay the same — comes up around drinks. That reasoning is set out in [should non-drinkers pay the same share](/en/blog/non-drinkers-bill-split/).

## 🔑 Handovers are what kills it

Pots disappear when the organiser moves teams or leaves. Cash in a drawer and the record inside one person's head means the balance loses its basis the moment it changes hands.

A handover isn't just the money. It's the current balance, the recent purchases, and who's currently in. With those three, the next person continues the same arrangement.

When the record lives in one place, handing over is showing someone a screen. Pass on an envelope of cash alone and your successor has no option but to take the balance on trust. **Never creating a situation that runs on trust alone is the one condition for keeping this going.** The longer someone handles small sums, the more the record protects them.
