When someone resigns or transfers, a handover list gets written. Live accounts, client contacts, where the shared folder lives.

One thing never makes that list: **the few dollars here and there still outstanding between colleagues**. Nobody thinks of it as a handover item, and it becomes uncollectable the moment that person stops coming in.

## A resignation puts a deadline on the debt

Small workplace advances drift because nothing forces them. You'll see each other tomorrow, so it doesn't have to be today — and that judgement renews itself daily.

Resignation destroys the premise. **What was fine whenever becomes due by a date.** Nothing changes about the amount or the relationship; only the existence of a deadline.

The awkward part is how quietly it happens. Everyone knows about the departure, but only the two people involved know about the debt. Nobody asks what you're doing about it, so there's no prompt, and the last day arrives.

## The send-off mood washes the money talk away

The final weeks run at a different temperature. Leaving drinks, a card going round, closing speeches. There's a strong pull towards ending things well.

In that atmosphere, money talk looks out of place. Invoicing someone you've worked alongside for years, in their final week, for fifteen dollars, feels emotionally uneconomic.

So most people swallow it. And the person who swallowed it is the only one still carrying the number. **The send-off mood doesn't erase the amount, it just makes it unsayable.** Months later, when it surfaces, the reason to get in touch has gone.

## It's harder to raise from the side that stays

The same debt is a completely different task depending on which end of it you're on.

The person leaving can say "let me square up what I owe" and it lands fine — they have a natural reason, because something is ending for them. The person staying, asking the leaver for money, has a much harder time. It looks like chasing someone out of the door.

There's a practical fix for the asymmetry: **the person leaving puts both directions on the table first, what they owe and what they're owed**. Present the list yourself and nobody who stays has to ask. Arranging it so that the weaker position never has to speak first is the version with the least friction.

## What gets handed over is the record, not the number

Settling looks like the job of fixing an amount. In fact something has to come before that: a list of what's actually outstanding.

**Build that list from memory and things will be missing.** Lunch six months ago, a cab fare in July, last week's drinks. The smaller the amount, the further down the recall queue it sits.

## Leave no number behind on the last day

The goal on the way out isn't recovering every penny. It's **leaving the memory of an amount with nobody at all**.

Writing off ten dollars is fine. But if you're writing it off, say so. Told "honestly, forget it", the other person can accept that and close the loop. Write it off silently and they either go on believing they still owe you or have forgotten entirely, and both leave a residue.

What ends when you leave a job is a contract and a desk. The memory of an unsettled amount outlasts both. Getting the number to zero before the last day is less an administrative task than the preparation that lets the relationship carry on in a different form.
