A parent goes into hospital, moves into a care home, or starts needing weekly appointments. The sibling who lives nearest handles it, because they are the one who can get there.

A few months later that same person is carrying every receipt. **The problem is rarely the amount. It is that months have passed with no record.**

## 🧓 Decide what counts as a cost before you split anything

Agree on the scope first. "Half each" means nothing while each of you is halving a different total.

The arguments happen at the edges. Care home fees and hospital bills are obviously shared. Replacement clothes, incontinence pads, the petrol to visit, a hotel night when you travel down: those read as a necessary cost to one sibling and a personal choice to another.

Sorting everything into three buckets at the start saves the argument later.

| Bucket | Examples | How it's treated |
|---|---|---|
| Shared by everyone | Care fees, medical bills, care supplies | Anything with an invoice |
| Carried by the spender | Your own travel, your own hotel | Visiting is your own call |
| Discussed each time | Appliances, home adaptations | Large enough to warrant a conversation |

## 🧮 Dividing by headcount penalises the nearest sibling

Three siblings, $1,200 a month, $400 each. Easy arithmetic, uneven reality.

Say the sister who lives ten minutes away takes your father to four appointments a month. Petrol and parking run to $60. On top of that, two hours disappear every trip. Neither figure ever reaches the spreadsheet. Six months of that and **the money is split evenly while the work is not, which is exactly how resentment starts.**

## 🚗 Put a price on the work, not just the receipts

The fix is to price the unpaid contribution and add it to the cost side. Gratitude does not divide; numbers do.

Take the same example. Add $60 of travel to four trips valued at $30 each, and her in-kind contribution is $180 a month.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Receipts (care, medical, supplies) | $1,200 |
| Sister's appointment trips (in kind) | $180 |
| Total | $1,380 |
| Each share | $460 |

She has already covered $180 of her own share, so she transfers $280. The other two send $460 each. The cash adds up to $280 + $460 + $460 = $1,200, which matches the receipts exactly.

There is no science behind valuing a trip at $30. It does not need any — a figure everyone agreed to works. That is the argument in [what fair splitting actually means](/en/blog/what-fair-splitting-means/): being decided in advance beats being objectively right.

## 📋 The record disappears faster than the money

Care costs differ from ordinary shared spending in one way: there is no end date. A holiday settles itself when everyone gets home. This runs for years.

So close the books monthly. Total it at month end, settle in the first days of the next one. Bill six months at once and you get a large number attached to expenses nobody remembers. It is the same mechanism as [a fixed settlement day for couples](/en/blog/monthly-settlement-day/): the interval matters more than the sum.

Keep the record somewhere everyone can open, not in the phone of whoever paid. [Set up a shared project](/en/docs/projects/) in Evere and the others can check any month for themselves. If the only way to find out is to ask, the person doing the caring also ends up doing the explaining.

## 🗂️ Keep inheritance out of this month's numbers

What actually derails these conversations is inheritance arriving in the middle of them.

"Does what I'm putting in now get taken into account later?" is a fair question. Ask it at the same time as the monthly split, though, and the monthly split stops happening. **Settle today's costs as today's costs, and hold the inheritance conversation when it is actually due.** Keeping them apart is what lets the arrangement survive.

Which is another reason to keep the record. It gives any future claim something to stand on; without it, both sides are arguing from memory.

## ❓ Common questions

**Q. What if the parent's own pension or savings covers some of it?**
Their money goes first, and siblings only split the shortfall. Make sure everyone can see who is managing that money, though. The worst outcome is the sibling doing the managing falling under suspicion.

**Q. One sibling earns far more than the others.**
Split by share rather than equally. The mechanics are the same as in [proportional splitting by income](/en/blog/income-based-split-couples/) — fix the ratio once, record it, and stop renegotiating every month.

**Q. One sibling simply won't pay.**
Chasing rarely works. Look instead at whether their share can be paid in kind: paperwork, phone calls with the care provider, one visit a month. If even that fails, record what happened and keep going rather than stalling the whole arrangement.

**Q. How many receipts do we actually need to keep?**
Keep anything large and anything that might be tax deductible. For small everyday items, the amount alone is enough. Demanding perfect documentation just loads more work onto the person already doing the most.
