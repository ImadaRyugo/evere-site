You're at their place half the week, but you haven't moved in. Talking about money in this arrangement is harder than it would be if you had.

Moving in gives you a natural opening: we live together now, let's divide the bills. Half living together has no such moment. **You can't claim to live there and you can't claim to be visiting**, so the conversation has no obvious place to begin.

## Only the increase is actually in question

To be clear about the principle first: what the visiting person should cover is the amount that exists because they're there. Not the costs that were happening anyway.

Whoever holds the lease pays the rent whether the other person shows up or not. That figure doesn't move by a single dollar. Meanwhile the shower runs more often, there's more laundry, and the fridge empties faster.

Mixing those two together is what makes this conversation go badly. **Separate the fixed costs from the variable ones first**, and everything after that is just arithmetic.

## Is asking for rent going too far?

Rent is where opinions genuinely split. If someone is there half the week, the argument that they should pay half the rent isn't unreasonable on its face.

Except that the visiting person is also paying rent on their own place. Contributing to both means paying for housing twice over. If you're going to fund your partner's rent, giving up your own flat and actually moving in is the more rational version of that.

Which means asking for rent **is inseparable from asking to move the relationship forward**. It looks like a conversation about money and it is really a conversation about living together. Raise the rent question without acknowledging that, and a financial negotiation quietly turns into a relationship negotiation, which goes badly.

The exception is when the arrangement has run for a long time and one flat has effectively become home. At that point including rent is the honest framing, and moving from there into a conversation about cohabiting is a natural next step.

## What actually goes up: utilities, food, supplies

Three things increase. Utilities, the food eaten at home, and consumables like toilet paper and detergent.

Food is the big one. Cooking dinner for two costs more in ingredients, and breakfast items disappear twice as fast. Utilities typically rise by tens of dollars a month; food climbs past a hundred without trying.

Supplies are small in money but they run out faster, so both the cost and the trips to replace them land on one person. Ignore them because the amounts look trivial and the person carrying that load still remembers the errands. Drawing the line around what counts as shared uses the same logic as [the grey area in shared groceries](/en/blog/groceries-gray-area-split/).

## Setting a frequency beats setting an amount

Calculating an exact figure every month is too heavy for this stage. Pick a method that won't survive and it stops after two months.

What works is a fixed monthly amount. Measure the increase once, round it to something clean, and pay that. When next month's number differs slightly, keep paying the same figure anyway.

The other approach is to divide by category instead of by money. The visiting person handles the grocery run, or covers more of the meals out. The burden shifts without anyone calculating anything, and **because no cash changes hands, the psychological resistance is much lower**.

## A month at three nights a week

Bo stays at Alex's place three nights a week, thirteen nights a month. Alex's rent is $900.

The costs that went up because Bo is there: $45 in utilities, $180 in food eaten together at home, and $35 in household supplies. That's $260.

| Approach | Bo pays |
|---|---|
| Nothing | $0 |
| Half the increase | $130 |
| All of the increase | $260 |
| Plus rent, prorated by nights | $455 |

Prorating rent gives 900 × 13/30 = $390, and half of that is $195, which takes the total to $455. Looking at that number, Bo would be paying $455 a month on top of their own rent, and the double housing cost becomes impossible to miss.

The realistic landing spots are the middle two. Which one depends on the income gap and how the time actually splits, and the reasoning in [three ways to divide living costs](/en/blog/cohabitation-expense-split/) transfers directly.

## Common questions

**They say they don't want anything. Should I still pay?**
A lot of people won't take cash, so pay it in shopping instead. Bring the groceries, restock the household supplies, cover more dinners out. It's the same money, and it meets almost none of the same resistance.

**The number of nights varies month to month.**
Keep the fixed amount anyway. Settling monthly means counting nights, and counting nights is its own kind of uncomfortable. Revisiting it every six months to check it still matches reality is enough.

**We stay at each other's places roughly equally.**
Then it cancels out and nobody needs to pay anything. The exception is when one flat is much bigger, or better located, so the nights skew heavily. Once the ratio passes about three to one, it's worth a conversation.

**When does this become a conversation about moving in?**
When one flat has clearly become home and your belongings have started migrating there. At that point paying rent twice is straightforwardly wasteful. The feeling that the money admin has become annoying is usually the signal, arriving slightly before the realisation does.
