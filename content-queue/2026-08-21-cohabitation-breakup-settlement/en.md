When a couple stops living together, money is the thing that gets pushed to the end of the list. There's the moving date, the logistics, the address changes. There's a lot to do, and settling up carries an emotional weight that none of the other tasks do.

But pushing it back means **you'll be negotiating it after you've already moved apart**. Agreeing figures with someone you no longer see is dramatically harder than finishing it while you're both still in the flat.

## 📦 There are three separate things to settle

What needs settling divides into three categories that behave differently.

The first is ordinary living costs that haven't been squared up: this month's groceries and utilities, still sitting on somebody's card. Small amounts, many of them, and impossible to reconstruct without records.

The second is things you bought together. Furniture, appliances, kitchenware, bedding. They exist as objects rather than money, so you have to decide who takes each one and at what price.

The third is money attached to the flat itself. The deposit, unused insurance, a lock change. None of these have a final number until after you've handed the keys back.

Try to discuss all three at once and you'll be arguing about settled and unsettled figures in the same conversation, which is how these stall.

## 🛋️ Don't value furniture at what you paid

Jointly bought items cause the most friction. You bought a $1,000 sofa together and one of you is keeping it. Does that mean handing over $500? No.

A two-year-old sofa isn't worth $1,000. It's worth roughly what someone would pay for it secondhand. So the figure that belongs in the settlement is **what it's worth now, not what it cost**.

The quickest way to find that is to look up the same model on a resale app. If that's too much effort, apply a flat residual rate by age instead: 70% after a year, 40% after two, 25% after three. Numbers that rough work perfectly well.

Whoever keeps the item pays the other half of its current value. Anything neither of you wants gets sold, and you split what it fetches. Disposal costs for the things that don't sell are split the same way.

## 🏠 The deposit isn't final until you've moved out

The deposit comes back to whoever signed the lease. Even if the name on it is one of yours, if you both funded the move-in costs, the refund belongs to both of you.

The complication is that you don't know the refund until after the final inspection. Deductions for damage come off it, so what you put down isn't necessarily what comes back. And once you're arguing about whose fault the scuff on the floor was, that becomes its own dispute.

The practical answer is to **carve the deposit out as a later settlement**. Finish everything else now, and split the refund once the number exists. Leaving one open item means the remaining contact is a single exchange when the money lands.

Where damage is clearly attributable to one person, deduct that from their side before splitting the rest. Smoke staining and pet damage are usually handled that way.

## 📅 Pick the date the household actually split

The awkward question for living costs is when shared costs stop. The day you decided, the day one of you moved out, the day the lease ended. There's more than one candidate.

Use **the date you actually stopped sharing a household**. Once someone isn't sleeping there, there's no case for them funding utilities or groceries they aren't using. Rent is the exception: it runs until the lease does, so it stays shared to the move-out date.

Fix that line first and the rest is just filtering the records. Skip it and every single entry turns into a separate question about whether it counts.

## 🧮 Two years, worked through

Furniture and appliances bought together came to $3,840. At two years and a 40% residual, that's $1,536 today. The deposit was $1,800 and $1,200 came back. Bo also owes Alex $320 in unsettled living costs. Alex is keeping the furniture.

| Item | Direction | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Buying out the furniture (half of current value) | Alex → Bo | $768 |
| Half the returned deposit (paid into Alex's account) | Alex → Bo | $600 |
| Unsettled living costs | Bo → Alex | $320 |

Net it out and Alex sends Bo $1,048 once, and that's the end of it: 768 + 600 − 320.

Paying each line separately means three transfers back and forth. Netting makes it one. Not having to message an ex about a payment three separate times is worth more than the money involved.

## ❓ Common questions

**What if we never kept records?**
Reconstructing two years isn't realistic, so limit it to the last month or two. Agreeing not to settle anything before that is healthier than fighting over conflicting memories. Pulling the large amounts off bank and card statements is worth doing.

**Can I claim back all the times I paid more?**
If you'd agreed to settle as you went, yes. Producing years of silent overpayment at the end is much harder to sustain, because from the other side you lent money without ever saying so. It works only where there's a running record, and then it's a statement of fact rather than a demand.

**What about things that were gifts?**
Gifts aren't part of the settlement. The difference from a joint purchase is what it was bought as at the time. Where that's genuinely unclear, the fair landing spot is that it goes to whoever actually used it, not whoever's card paid.

**The lease is in one name and that person is leaving.**
You'll need to talk to the letting agent, because the contract has to change. If a transfer isn't possible you're looking at ending and restarting the tenancy, which triggers fresh move-in costs. Who covers those is a settlement item, and one to agree before it appears.
