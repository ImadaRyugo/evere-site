Eat out with friends in Japan and you'll often see this: after the bill comes, someone says "3,000 yen each," and everyone hands over that amount. The Japanese word for this is 割り勘 (warikan), splitting the bill. Among friends it's completely ordinary, and suggesting it carries almost no awkwardness.

How the bill itself works, though, has some distinctly Japanese features. Not knowing them can leave you confused at the register, or feeling you've been charged more than you expected.

## One payment per table is the default

In many Japanese restaurants, you don't pay at the table. When you're done, you take the bill to the register near the exit. If there's no bill on the table, call a staff member and say "okaikei onegai shimasu" (the check, please).

The thing to know is that **many places expect a single payment per table**. You can ask to pay separately with "betsubetsu de onegai shimasu," but busy izakayas may say no. That's why groups in Japan often have one person pay and collect from everyone afterward. The person who takes on that role is called the 幹事 (kanji, the organizer; it just happens to sound the same as the word for Chinese characters).

## The izakaya otoshi goes into the split

At an izakaya, a small dish you didn't order may appear in front of each person. It's called お通し (otoshi), and in most cases it adds a charge of a few hundred yen per person to the bill. Think of it as a kind of table charge.

To a first-timer it can look like an unrequested extra, but it's a standard part of izakaya dining. The bill total already includes it, so when splitting, just divide that total by the headcount.

## No tipping

Japan has no tipping culture, whether at restaurants or in taxis. Leave money on the table and staff may even chase you down thinking you forgot it. The total on the bill is exactly what you pay, which makes splitting simpler than in countries where you have to work out a tip. For how groups handle tips elsewhere, see [Who Pays the Tip? Splitting It, or Keeping It Personal](/en/blog/tipping-and-splitting/).

## The organizer rounds to a clean number

The per-person amount is often rounded to a tidy unit like 1,000 or 500 yen. With a 17,800 yen bill for six people, rather than collecting 2,967 yen each, the organizer collects 3,000 yen from everyone and either carries the extra 200 yen over to the next get-together or gives it back to someone who paid a little more.

**Collecting quickly matters more than splitting to the last yen**, and that's characteristic of how Japanese groups split.

## At work events, seniors pay more

At a dinner with colleagues, not everyone pays the same. Managers and senior staff often put in more and junior staff less. Sometimes the most senior person pays the whole thing.

If someone treats you or pays more than their share, say **"gochisōsama deshita" (thank you for the meal) as you leave**. Thanking them again the next day comes across as especially polite.

## Cash or PayPay, with a catch for visitors

In Japan, a lot of bill-splitting still happens in cash, with everyone handing notes to the organizer. For phone transfers, an app called PayPay is widely used.

However, **opening a PayPay account requires a Japanese mobile number**. Short-term visitors can't send money to Japanese friends through PayPay. Some home-country payment apps can be used to pay at shops, but that's different from sending money to a friend. LINE Pay, once used for transfers, ended its service in Japan in 2025.

If you're a visitor splitting with Japanese friends, cash is the most reliable way. If you're traveling with friends from home, the practical approach is for one person to pay by card and settle up later with the apps you already use.

## Common questions

**Q. Do couples split the bill on dates in Japan?**
It depends on the couple. Plenty of couples split, while some people still feel the man should pay more. If you're unsure, follow your partner's lead or talk about it early on.

**Q. Is it rude to suggest splitting the bill?**
Not among friends. In Japan, asking "warikan de ii?" (shall we split it?) before the bill comes is completely normal.

**Q. If I lend or borrow money with a Japanese friend, which currency should we settle in?**
Record what was spent in Japan in yen, then convert to one of your currencies when you settle up. Agree on when to convert, or exchange rate swings will change the amounts.
