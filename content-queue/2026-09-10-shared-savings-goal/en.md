Couples moving in together usually get as far as agreeing how to split the bills. Then the energy runs out, and the conversation about savings gets pushed to next year.

But **the moment you settle how living costs are divided, you've half-settled the savings too**, because that decision determines what each of you has left. Fix the savings figure without looking at that, and one of you quietly ends up squeezed.

## 🏦 Splitting costs and splitting savings are different questions

Treating them as one conversation is why the conversation goes nowhere. Living costs are about dividing what leaves this month. Savings are about what happens to what remains. There's an order.

Living costs come first. Evenly, by income, or by category. Choosing between those is covered in [the guide to splitting living costs](/en/blog/cohabitation-expense-split/).

Only then do you look at what's left, and only then can you talk about how much goes into joint savings. Reverse the order and you'll be setting a savings figure and then adjusting living costs to fit it, which almost always trims the lower earner.

## 🧮 Income-based costs plus equal savings widens the gap

Words don't carry this, so here are numbers. A takes home $2,800, B takes home $2,000, and their living costs are $2,400 a month.

Split by income, A pays $1,400 and B pays $1,000. So far it looks fair. The question is what happens when $600 a month goes into joint savings.

| How savings are split | A saves | B saves | A keeps | B keeps |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Equally | $300 | $300 | $1,100 | $700 |
| By income | $350 | $250 | $1,050 | $750 |

The equal row is 2,800 − 1,400 − 300 = $1,100 against 2,000 − 1,000 − 300 = $700, a gap of $400. The income row is 2,800 − 1,400 − 350 = $1,050 against 2,000 − 1,000 − 250 = $750, a gap of $300.

**Weighting the bills and then saving equally cancels out part of the weighting you just agreed to.** If costs go by ratio, savings should use the same ratio. Doing both equally is also a coherent choice; it's splitting one way and saving the other that leaves you half-committed.

## 🎯 Joint savings without a purpose don't survive

Harder than setting the amount is keeping it running, and the reason is consistent: nobody wrote down what it's for.

Savings that start as "we'll need it at some point" stop within about six months. One person skips a month after an unexpected bill, the other stops the month after, and the balance sits still while nobody has done anything wrong.

The couples who keep it going have three things written down: what for, how much, and by when. Something like "moving costs in two years, $6,000" makes the monthly figure fall out of the arithmetic: 6,000 over 24 months is $250. With a purpose in place, the amount stops being something to negotiate and becomes a result.

## 🔐 Keep joint savings separate from personal savings

Money aimed at a shared goal and money each of you holds for yourself should sit in different accounts with different records.

The reason is how heavy it is to have your balance visible to somebody else. Share personal balances and whoever has more gets an unspoken expectation to top up the joint pot, while whoever has less gets asked to explain. **The only balance that needs sharing is the joint one.**

Keep the joint savings away from the day-to-day account too. Money parked where the bills come out gets spent in any month that runs short. Putting it somewhere untouchable is the mechanism. For running a monthly cycle with a fixed settlement date, see [the monthly settlement day](/en/blog/monthly-settlement-day/).

## 📄 Decide the split if you separate, before you start

Saving jointly before marriage makes this unavoidable. It feels like inviting bad luck, and skipping it guarantees an argument later.

Two decisions. Does the money come back in the proportions you put it in, or half each? And if you stop partway to the goal, who decides what happens to it? If you contributed by income ratio, returning it the same way is the natural answer.

The account will be in one name, so **make the deposit record carry the fact that the name on it isn't the same as ownership**. With who paid in what and when on record, either name works. For separating a shared home generally, see [settling up when you stop living together](/en/blog/cohabitation-breakup-settlement/).

## ❓ Common questions

**Q. The income gap is large and one of us can barely save.**
Lower the joint contribution. If the two of you together can't reach the goal, revise the goal rather than the contribution. An unrealistic figure doesn't just fail; it leaves the person who couldn't keep up feeling they let it fail.

**Q. Whose name should the joint account be in?**
Whichever is administratively simpler. The decision that matters isn't the name, it's the withdrawal rule: can either of you take money out alone, or does it need both? Set that by the size of the balance.

**Q. Do bonuses go in at the same ratio?**
Handle them separately from the monthly amount. Bonuses differ in size and timing between people, and folding them into a ratio makes the calculation fiddly. Deciding case by case when extra money arrives is enough.

**Q. What happens to the joint savings after marriage?**
The mechanism carries over unchanged. What changes is the purpose, from moving costs to housing or education. When the purpose changes, rewrite the amount and the deadline at the same time.
