The annoying part of getting home from a work trip isn't the expense claim. It's that **what you claim from the company and what you split with colleagues both left the same wallet**.

Train and hotel: company. Dinner: personal. The taxi you shared three ways: personal, split. Try to sort that from memory and something always goes missing.

## 💼 A work trip runs on two wallets

Spending on a trip is two different things wearing the same clothes. There's what the company reimburses under policy, and what colleagues settle between themselves.

They differ in who you settle with, by when, and what proof is needed. The company wants receipts and a form; your colleague wants nothing but agreement on a number. Manage them as one pile and you get a record that satisfies neither requirement.

One test is enough: is it in the expense policy? If yes, company. If no, personal. Anything you're unsure about is safer on the personal side — splitting something you could have claimed costs you nothing, while claiming something you shouldn't is a policy breach.

## 🧾 Receipts go to the company and never come back

The practical snag is where the proof ends up. Receipts you submit don't return. If your company takes originals, nothing stays in your hands.

That bites when a colleague split is buried inside a claimable receipt. Four of you eat, two covered as client entertainment, two paying their own way — that happens constantly. Once the receipt is filed, "what was my share again" has no answer.

There's one fix: **record the amount somewhere else before you hand it in**. In Evere you can [attach an image when you log the expense](/en/docs/expenses/), so photographing the receipt before submitting leaves you both the total and the breakdown.

## 🚕 Shared taxis and shared rooms need the rule said out loud

The two classic sources of colleague-to-colleague splits are sharing a cab and sharing a room.

Taxis get unfair by drop-off order. Paying the same as everyone when you got out first genuinely does grate. But distance-weighting turns a $30 ride into arithmetic homework, so **fixing it as $30 three ways, $10 each, is the version that actually gets used**.

Rooms are a different matter. Where the company caps accommodation per person, sharing can put the real cost below that cap. Splitting the difference between yourselves is not allowed under many policies. Treat a shared room as the company's business and leave it out of the personal pot.

The underlying logic for dividing a vehicle by heads is laid out in [splitting the cost of a rental car trip](/en/blog/rental-car-trip-costs/).

## 📆 The company deadline and the personal one don't match

Expense claims have a cycle. Month-end cut-off with payment the following month is standard, and three weeks from filing to money landing is unremarkable.

The colleague split has no such grace. Unless it closes while the trip is fresh, nobody can verify the numbers any more.

| Category | Settled with | Realistic deadline |
|---|---|---|
| Company expenses | The company | The policy cut-off |
| Personal splits | Colleagues | Within a week of getting back |

**Closing the personal side first is the correct order.** A claim with complete paperwork processes fine weeks later; human memory is vague within seven days. The everyday version of this closing habit is in [covering lunch at work](/en/blog/workplace-lunch-advance/).

## 🎁 Souvenirs aren't an expense

The overlooked item is what you bring back for the team. At most companies that isn't claimable.

It turns into a problem when the custom outlives any decision about who pays. If the same person buys every time, that person is quietly absorbing a few hundred dollars a year.

Two ways to make it explicit: collect for it as a team cost, or drop the custom. Continuing without deciding is the worst of the three. If you make it a team cost, it works the same way as [office collections for weddings and funerals](/en/blog/workplace-condolence-money/) — settle the process first.

## ❓ Common questions

**Q. Whose name goes on the receipt?**
The company's for claimable items, nobody's for personal ones. Where one receipt contains both, ask for separate bills at the till if you can.

**Q. A colleague has forgotten the fare I covered.**
Raise it when you file the trip report. Attaching it to a work milestone makes it administration rather than chasing.

**Q. What about mixed currencies on an overseas trip?**
Record what you paid in the currency you paid it in. The rate your company applies and the rate you use between yourselves may not match. Face value keeps both options open.

**Q. My manager paid for dinner on a two-person trip.**
Log it, don't settle it. Whether it went on the company card or their own is sometimes better left unasked. The fact alone is enough for the next decision.
