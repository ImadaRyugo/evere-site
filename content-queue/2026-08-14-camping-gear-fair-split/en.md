If you only look at the cash that leaves people's pockets on the day, a camping trip is always lopsided. Split the pitch fee and the food evenly and go home is the usual approach, and it leaves the person who brought the tent out of pocket every single time.

Nothing came out of their wallet at the campsite, but **they already paid for this trip** — a few years ago, in one lump.

## 🏕️ Gear isn't free, it depreciates

Tent, tarp, fire pit, lantern, cool box, table and chairs. A full kit is a serious amount of money, but spread across the trips it will do, the per-trip number is small.

The method is just **purchase price divided by how many times it'll get used**. A $300 tent over thirty trips is $10 a night. At that scale, sharing it costs nobody anything they'll notice.

You don't need to be accurate. Ask the owner what a night feels worth to them and use that figure. Owners consistently undervalue their own kit, so taking their number at face value tends to end the discussion rather than start one.

## 🔥 Separate the consumables from the assets

Camping kit divides into two kinds of thing: what gets used up, and what comes home and goes out again.

Charcoal, firewood, gas canisters, firelighters, food, ice. These are gone by Sunday, so whoever bought them fronted a cost and the full amount gets split. Nothing unusual here, it's an ordinary shared purchase.

Tents and cookware are assets, so splitting their full price is obviously too much. **Only the value of tonight's use becomes a number**, which is what depreciation means in practice. Agree that line and every other figure follows from it.

The awkward middle is things like pegs and a half-used gas canister. The amounts are small enough that you should just treat them as consumables and move on.

## 🚙 The invisible work is hauling and pitching

The person who owns the gear is usually also the person who transports it. They give up their boot space, leave earlier, and do the first hour of work when everyone arrives.

Whether that labour should become money is genuinely arguable, but **settling the cash fairly doesn't mean the trip is now even**. Splitting the fuel for the haul, or making a rule that the owner doesn't take anything down at the end, does more real work than a payment would.

Push too much of this into money and camping starts to feel transactional. Pricing the gear and repaying the rest in roles is where most groups end up settling.

## 💰 Rental prices give you a shortcut

If setting a depreciation figure yourselves feels tedious, look up a hire shop's nightly rate. You'll have a price for a tent kit and a fire pit within a minute.

Take that number as-is and the owner does slightly well out of it, because rental prices include the shop's margin and their logistics. **Somewhere between half and 70% of the rental rate** is closer to how it actually feels. The owner is hauling and pitching it themselves, so the fair level sits above free and below a shop.

## 🧾 A four-person example

One night with an $80 pitch fee and $120 of food, charcoal and gas, so $200 of actual cash. Alex brought the tent, tarp and fire pit, which you value at $60 based on local hire rates.

Splitting only the cash gives $50 each. Now add the gear: $60 across four people is $15 each, and Alex collects the other three shares, $45.

| | Cash split | Gear settlement | Actually pays |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alex (brought gear) | $50 | −$45 | $5 |
| Bo | $50 | +$15 | $65 |
| Chris | $50 | +$15 | $65 |
| Dana | $50 | +$15 | $65 |

The total is 5 + 65 × 3 = $200, matching the cash that actually left. Valuing the gear doesn't create money, it just moves the burden between four people, so the total never changes.

If Alex ending up at $5 feels wrong, the gear valuation is too high. Drop it to $30 and Alex pays $27.50 while the others pay $57.50 each. **Moving the number until it feels right** gets you to an answer far faster than debating fairness in the abstract.
