Visiting family looks like travel and is accounted for nothing like it. A holiday gets a budget beforehand and a settle-up afterwards. Family visits get neither.

Repeat that for a few years and **money quietly flows towards one side's family without anyone noticing.**

## Whose expense is a family visit anyway?

Start with whose errand it is.

Going to see your own parents is your errand, which argues for paying your own fare. But when you live with someone and you both go to one side's family, the person coming along isn't running their own errand. Apply "whoever's family, whoever pays" and the companion's fare is left hanging.

The workable line is this: **treat visiting fares as travel spending from the shared budget**, then look at the difference in distance and frequency over a whole year. Arguing about each trip tells you less than one annual total does.

## Matching the number of trips doesn't make it fair

Take a concrete case. His parents are a $180 round trip away; hers are $20. You go to his three times a year and hers six. By count, her family wins two to one.

| Destination | Round trip each | Trips a year | Yearly cost for two |
|---|---|---|---|
| His parents | $180 | 3 | $1,080 |
| Her parents | $20 | 6 | $240 |
| Total | | | $1,320 |

Her side gets twice the visits; his side costs 4.5 times as much. Decide to "go to each family the same number of times" and the household simply spends more. Equal trip counts are not equal anything.

Paid from the shared budget, that $1,320 is split between you: $660 each, or by ratio if your incomes differ, on the same logic as [proportional splitting by income](/en/blog/income-based-split-couples/). **Neither of you chose where the other was born.**

## When a parent offers to pay the fare

If the journey is long, parents often offer to cover it. Refusing every time and accepting every time both cause problems later.

What actually needs deciding is where the money lands. Does it go into the shared budget, or into the pocket of whoever was handed it? Skip that conversation and it starts to look like one of you personally profits from visits to their own family.

If the shared budget paid the fare, the refund belongs to the shared budget. If it was a personal trip, it's personal money. Obvious once said out loud, and a mess until it is.

## Gifts and spending during the stay need a boundary

Gifts are harder to handle than fares, because the amounts are small and they recur.

In practice, fold gifts into the cost of the visit. A $25 box of something nine times a year is $225. Leave it out and the same person keeps doing the buying, which means the same person keeps carrying it.

Spending during the stay is a separate question: who pays when you all eat out, what to do about money handed to grandchildren. The amount that really escalates is the one where parents insist on covering every meal for everyone. Lean on that and the next visit is awkward. Once it has happened a few times, say in advance that you'll take one of the meals. It is easier to offer beforehand than to argue at the till.

## When siblings gather, name whose budget it is

The moment siblings converge for a memorial or a holiday, the source of the money goes vague. Do the parents pay, do the visiting households split it, does whoever lives nearby front it?

What actually happens is that **the sibling who lives nearby does the shopping, fronts the cash, and then never bills anyone.** It isn't about the amount. There is simply no moment to raise it before everyone leaves.

The fix is to decide who covers what before people arrive. The procedure is the one in [splitting a parent's care costs between siblings](/en/blog/sibling-elder-care-costs/): define the scope first, divide second. Log the shopping in Evere and settling up is a matter of turning the screen around.

## Common questions

**Q. Won't putting fares in the shared budget make it awkward to visit the family that's further away?**
The opposite. Unspoken restraint is what happens when nobody knows the number. Once the annual figure is visible, the conversation becomes how many trips fit the budget.

**Q. How do we count the children's fares?**
If the family travels, every seat goes into the cost of the visit. Costs climb steeply with each child, which makes knowing the annual figure more useful, not less.

**Q. What about driving there?**
Put in fuel and tolls at cost. Hire cars or car clubs are treated the same way. As for the driving itself, [splitting the cost of a hire car](/en/blog/rental-car-trip-costs/) covers the question of whether to price it, and deciding that in advance avoids the argument later.

**Q. Our gift budget creeps up every time.**
Set a ceiling and choose within it. The amount needn't be identical each visit, but with no ceiling there's a quiet pressure never to turn up with something cheaper than last time, and the number only goes one way.
