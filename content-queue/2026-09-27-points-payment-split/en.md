An $80 bill, $30 of it paid with points. Only $50 left an account.

Splitting four ways, is the total $80 or $50? **Is the $30 in points money somebody paid, or money that fell out of the sky?** Until that's answered there's no per-person figure.

## Points are money already spent, wearing a different shape

Start with the fact. Points didn't appear for free. Somebody bought something earlier and was issued them in proportion to what they spent.

Points are **past spending coming back in a different form**. What separates them from cash is when they were paid, not who paid them. Get that straight and the handling follows almost automatically.

You don't need to deliver that argument at the table. What matters is that adopting "points are free" as a premise makes everything calculated after it wrong.

## Treating them as zero breaks the next occasion

Count the points portion as nothing and the evening settles neatly. Divide $50 four ways, $12.50 each. The person who spent the points and the other three all appear to have lost nothing.

The breakage shows up next time. That person distributed something generated by their own past spending across the whole table, and no record of the distribution exists anywhere. When the same situation comes round, they don't use points. **A contribution nobody registered the first time doesn't get offered a second time.**

Structurally this is identical to a coupon somebody brought along. Whether a perk someone supplies becomes everybody's gain is covered in [whose saving is a discount on the bill](/en/blog/coupon-discount-split/).

## Treating them as cash only costs the person who had them

Take the opposite approach and count points exactly as cash.

Divide $80 four ways, $20 each. The person who spent points covered $30 of it, so the other three hand them $10 between them. The arithmetic is right, and their earlier spending is properly recognised.

There's one place it grates, though. **Points can't be handed to anyone else.** With cash you can simply give the $30 back; someone who paid in points receives cash instead. In effect they converted an asset from points into money, and almost nobody experiences that as unfair. If anything it's the more honest outcome.

## Separate who paid from who owes

The confusion comes from arguing about the payment method and the liability on the same footing. They come apart cleanly.

The method can be cash, card or points. All that's recorded is that somebody covered the bill at the time. Who owes what is a separate question about how that bill divides.

That's how it goes into Evere as well: [log one expense and pick the participants](/en/docs/expenses/), with nothing asking how it was paid. Total $80, one person fronted it, four participants, and $20 each falls out. Whether points were involved doesn't affect whether the figure is right. **What belongs in the record is how much value moved through that bill, not which wallet it left.**

## Points about to expire are the one fair exception

That's the principle. There's a single case worth excepting: points that vanish if unused.

Points near expiry are already worth close to nothing to their holder. Someone saying "they were about to go anyway, don't worry about it" is being perfectly rational, and there's no reason for anyone to feel awkward accepting.

**But that call belongs to the person who held them.** If everyone else decides on their behalf that the points were expiring, it stops being a contribution and becomes a collection. Who owns points is also the subject of [is it unfair for the organiser to keep the points](/en/blog/organizer-points-debate/), and the conclusion is the same one: the person who brought it decides, and everybody else goes along.

What splitting a bill is ultimately tested on isn't whether the figure is right to the cent. It's whether something one person put in stays visible as something they put in. Points are simply a payment method where the amount is hard to see, which makes that recognition unusually easy to lose.
