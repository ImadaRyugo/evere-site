"Sorry, I've left my wallet" is nothing the first time. By the third time, you notice your own manner has changed, and you notice it before they do.

The awkward part is that there's no bad intent. They aren't dodging anything, they genuinely forgot. So there's nothing to be annoyed about, and you end up as the only person carrying the running total in your head. This is about clearing that state without damaging the friendship.

## 👛 The absence of bad intent is what delays the fix

If someone obviously had no plan to pay you back, the decision would be easy. Step back, or stop covering them, and you're done.

It's the opposite case that drags on. The nicer the person, the longer this lasts, because "not them" is exactly what makes you postpone raising it, and postponing makes it harder to raise. **The longer it runs, the higher the difficulty of mentioning it climbs, while the amount stays where it was.** That asymmetry is the whole problem.

So the fix isn't aimed at their character, it's aimed at the procedure. Don't start doubting them. Just take the job of remembering out of your own head, and most friendships snap back to normal on their own.

## 🧾 Decide the repayment method, not the amount

Standing at the till, the thing to settle isn't how much you're covering. That number is already fixed. What you're deciding is when and how it comes back.

Keep it short. "I'll get this, send it over later" puts the method inside the sentence. Say nothing about method and wave it off, and they get to choose the route, which almost always turns into cash the next time you happen to meet, the slowest option available.

If they can transfer it there and then, that's the best outcome. Their phone is already out. Push a thirty-second job into next week and you'll both walk into the following dinner with only a rough idea of the figure.

## 🧮 Six months laid out looks different

Any single instance feels too small to chase. Lined up over half a year, they stop being small.

| Where you covered them | Each time | Times | Running total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lunch | $12 | 12 | $144 |
| Topping up the dinner bill | $40 | 3 | $120 |
| Corner shop and fares | $5 | 20 | $100 |

That's $144 + $120 + $100, or $364 in total. Every line on its own was an amount you'd shrug at.

The table isn't an argument that you must invoice them. It's the observation that **judging each instance separately means nobody ever looks at the sum**, and six months goes by. Look at the number first, then decide whether to let it go. Putting up with something without knowing its size is the most draining version of this.

## 🚧 If it repeats, change the order rather than the person

For someone this happens to over and over, a system beats a talking-to. All it takes is moving payment earlier.

Pick places where you pay before you sit down: counter service, prepaid bookings, events where everyone pays their own ticket at reservation time. If the setting doesn't function without your own card, the act of covering someone never occurs. As a way to stop the repeat without blaming anyone, this is the most reliable one there is.

In a group, it also helps to spread payments across the evening rather than putting them on one person: whoever gets the food, someone else gets the drinks, someone else the taxi. Everyone ends up on the paying side at least once, so no single person becomes the permanent forgetter. On whether small sums are worth mentioning at all, [is it cheap to chase small amounts](/en/blog/small-amounts-cheap/) covers the reasoning.

## 📱 Get the remembering out of your head

What ultimately makes this lighter is simply having a record. Once the figure is visible, chasing becomes a choice you can make later rather than a thing you have to decide now.

[Log the expense and pick who shares it](/en/docs/expenses/) in Evere and what you've covered stays there. Nobody else has to see it. Whether you can say "oh, while I remember" next time you meet depends on having a record, not on having a good memory.

For the wording when you do raise it, the templates in [how to ask a friend to pay you back](/en/blog/asking-friends-to-pay-back/) still apply. Checking the number before you write makes the message shorter too.

## ❓ Common questions

**Q. The amounts are small and now they've piled up unmentioned.**
Don't invoice them one by one. Give the total, once. "A bunch of small ones have added up to about $360, let's clear it in one go" lets the size of the number do the explaining, and it lands better than chasing each coffee.

**Q. What if they say they don't remember owing that much?**
Show the dates and the venues. Moving the discussion from amounts to facts usually ends it there. Without a record, both of you are arguing from memory, and that argument has no finish line.

**Q. It feels like the friendship lasts longer if I just let it go.**
That's a legitimate choice. Just remove it from the record at the moment you decide, so it becomes a gift rather than an outstanding balance. A loan you've silently written off but still think about is the version that lingers longest.

**Q. Can I get asked to cover people less often?**
Yes, by proposing the format before anyone asks. One question as you sit down, "can we pay separately here?", gives anyone without a wallet the moment to say so. Before ordering is a far easier place to say it than at the counter.
