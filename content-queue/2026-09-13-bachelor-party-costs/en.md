A friend is getting married, so everyone goes away together one more time before the wedding. It's a lovely thing to plan and a surprisingly awkward thing to collect money for.

The cause is almost always the same. **Nobody says out loud who covers the guest of honour, and the booking goes ahead anyway.** Leave it unstated and you discover on the day that all eight of you were working from different assumptions.

## 🎊 Put the answer in the first line of the invitation

There are three options for the guest of honour: pay the same as everyone, pay nothing at all, or pay only their own direct costs. Any of them is fine. **What matters is that the choice appears in the first message you send.**

Put it above the dates and the destination, because it's information people need in order to decide whether they're coming. Announcing later that the guest of honour's share will be divided up wrecks the budget of everyone who already did their sums.

Check with the guest of honour, too. Plenty of people are uncomfortable having everything paid for them. The flow that stalls an evening most reliably is the bride or groom trying to pay for things on the day because they didn't want anyone put out.

## 🧮 What the three options actually cost

Take eight people, two nights, $2,000 all in: $960 on accommodation, $640 on food, $400 on activities.

| How it's split | Guest of honour | Each of the other seven |
|---|---|---|
| Everyone pays the same | $250 | $250 |
| Guest of honour pays nothing | $0 | $285.71 |
| Guest of honour pays own costs only | $200 | $257.14 |

Equal is 2,000 ÷ 8 = $250. Comping them means dividing the whole thing by the remaining seven: 2,000 ÷ 7 = $285.71. The third row has the guest of honour cover their share of accommodation and food, 1,600 ÷ 8 = $200, with the remaining $1,800 split seven ways at $257.14.

The number to notice is that **comping the guest of honour adds about $36 a head**. That isn't much money, and going ahead without mentioning it leaves everyone with the impression that the trip cost more than they were told. Say it first and nobody minds at all. What produces the resentment is not the difference but not having been told about it.

## 📉 The person who suggested it shouldn't set the budget

Trips like this inflate as they get planned. One more night while we're at it, somewhere nicer because it's a special occasion, a photographer. Each decision is small and the total quietly reaches one and a half times the original.

There's one reliable way to stop it: collect the ceiling before you choose anything. Ask everyone anonymously what they can spend, and set the cap at the lowest answer. Nobody has to drop out. The procedure is the same one in [splitting costs on a graduation trip](/en/blog/graduation-trip-budget/), and it works just as well for people with salaries.

Decide in advance how extras get handled too. Anything above the cap gets paid for only by the people who want it. Once that rule exists, you don't have to talk anyone out of suggesting things.

## 🙅 Leave people a way to decline

This is an unusually hard event to say no to. A friend gets married once, so even somebody who genuinely can't afford it struggles to say so.

That means the organisers have to build the exit. Include a day people can join for, keep a first-night-only option, arrange a dinner later for whoever can't travel. **When there are several options, nobody has to explain why they picked the cheap one.**

The same applies to a group present. Letting people who can't travel join in on the gift instead splits the financial load across two events. For running that collection, see [collecting money for a group gift](/en/blog/group-gift-collection/).

## 📝 Have two organisers, not one

A practical note to finish on. Run a trip this size alone and booking, collecting and shepherding people all land on the same person.

Split it: one books and pays, one collects and settles. With two of you, the trip survives one of you falling ill. For designing a ticketed event generally, see [setting an after-party fee that breaks even](/en/blog/wedding-afterparty-accounting/), and for late drop-outs, [who pays when someone no-shows](/en/blog/cancellation-fee-who-pays/).

Accommodation and activities hit their cancellation deadlines early, so fix the date numbers are confirmed and tell everyone. **That date is the single most important line in the whole plan.**
