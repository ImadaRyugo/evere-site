The first surprise of organizing a class reunion is how long it lasts. A group dinner is a few days of work around the event. A reunion can run for the better part of six months.

All that time, money trickles out. A venue deposit, invitations, the effort to track down classmates. Every bit of it is meant to come back through ticket sales, but **tickets are paid at the very end, so for months it piles up as the organizer's advance.**

## 🏫 Reunion money starts moving six months out

Lay out the rough timeline and the order money leaves becomes clear.

Six months out, you book the venue and pay a deposit. Three months out, invitations go out, with printing, postage or event-site fees. A month out, you reach out to former teachers and arrange a gift. On the night, you collect and pay the venue. Afterward, you report back.

Every payment before the night is someone's advance. And until ticket money comes in, nobody actually knows how much has been spent in total.

## ✉️ Invitations and finding people cost money before anyone RSVPs

The cost unique to reunions is reaching people. The more years since graduation, the more classmates you have lost track of. Mailing to parents' old addresses, chasing people through mutual friends, printing name tags and a slideshow for a headcount you do not have yet.

None of that money comes back, however many people show up. So when you set the ticket price, add these prep costs divided by the expected attendance on top of the venue's per-head cost. **Price tickets on the venue alone and the prep costs come straight out of the organizer's pocket.** When attendance is hard to predict, the break-even thinking in [setting a ticket price that breaks even](/en/blog/wedding-afterparty-accounting/) applies.

## 🧑‍🤝‍🧑 With a committee, the advances scatter

A whole-class reunion usually has a committee rather than one organizer. More hands help, but on the money side, they create a problem.

A pays the venue deposit, B the printing, C the postage, D the teachers' gifts. Each pays from their own pocket and keeps their own receipt. On the night, when you try to total who paid what, one receipt is missing.

With a committee, start with one rule: **pick one treasurer, and record every advance on the day it happens.** In Evere, put the whole committee in one project and everyone sees who paid for what as it happens, with receipt photos attached.

## 💐 Who covers the former teachers you invite?

At reunions that invite former teachers, it is common not to charge them. That means their meals, and any flowers or gifts for them, are covered by everyone else's tickets.

What matters is saying so up front. **Make it clear in the invitation that the ticket price includes the teachers' share.** Ask for more on the night and you throw off everyone's budget. Sharing the cost of an invited guest works the same way as in [who covers the guest of honour](/en/blog/bachelor-party-costs/).

## 📮 The final report closes the organizers' advances

After the reunion, report the numbers and repay the committee. Here is an example with 48 paying classmates and two former teachers as guests.

| Type | Item | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Income | Tickets, $75 × 48 | $3,600 |
| Expense | Venue, food and drinks, $60 × 50 (incl. $500 deposit A paid in advance) | $3,000 |
| Expense | Printing and name tags (B and C) | $350 |
| Expense | Gifts for two teachers (D) | $200 |
| Net | Surplus | $50 |

The treasurer repays A, B, C and D from the ticket money. **Do the report and the repayments on the same day, and the organizers' advances close right there.**

Say in the report what happens to the surplus: carried forward for the next reunion, or spent on a thank-you drink for the committee. Even a small amount with no stated destination becomes the reason the next organizer, years later, gets asked what happened to the leftover money.
