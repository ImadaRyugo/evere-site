Once "I'll get this one" becomes a habit at work, the amount stays small while the conversation gets harder to start.

Seven dollars. Twice a week. Not worth chasing. But make that same call every week and you are past $150 by the third month, at a point where nobody can reconstruct which lunches it came from.

## 🍱 Small amounts get heavy by count, not by size

What makes daily advances awkward is that every single one, looked at alone, lands on "never mind". Messaging someone over $7 genuinely isn't worth it.

The problem is how fast that verdict accumulates. **The weight of one item and the weight of the pile grow on separate curves.** The money adds up linearly; the difficulty of remembering climbs much faster than that. By the tenth item, nobody can itemise it accurately.

So the fix isn't deciding what amount is worth asking for. It's **deciding how many items you let pile up**. Drop the threshold in money, set one in days or counts, and the judgement call disappears.

## 🔁 Why taking turns breaks down

The usual workplace answer is alternating. You get today, I'll get tomorrow. No record needed, settled on the spot, and it looks perfectly reasonable.

It breaks because people don't go out at the same rate. Client visits, meetings that run long, days someone brings food from home — the rotation stops landing evenly. On top of that the bill differs every time. A noodle place and a proper sit-down meal are $3 apart, and ten of those is a $30 lean in one direction.

Alternating works when **the group is fixed, the frequency matches, and the price range is narrow**. Break any one of those three and keeping a record is the easier path.

## 📅 Pick one day a week and close the books

The single most effective move is deciding in advance when you settle. Friday before you leave, Monday morning, either is fine. Fixing it to a weekday turns asking for money from "chasing that person" into "this week's close".

A week is the right unit. Daily means too many rounds; monthly lets enough items build up that nobody remembers them.

| Cycle | Items that build up | Suits |
|---|---|---|
| Every time | 1 | People you see occasionally |
| Weekly | 2–5 | Colleagues you see daily |
| Monthly | 8–20 | Larger amounts, low frequency |

The same closing-date logic runs household finances too. The monthly version is in [a settlement day stops the arguments](/en/blog/monthly-settlement-day/).

## 🧾 One person keeping the record is enough

Ask everyone to log their share and someone will not do it. At work especially, people avoid opening a personal finance app at their desk.

The record holds up if the person who paid is the one keeping it. [Log one expense and pick who it's split between](/en/docs/expenses/) in Evere and each person's share comes out on its own. On closing day, the [settlement screen](/en/docs/settlement/) gives you the figure you'd have had to work out by hand.

Having the numbers first changes the wording too. "Can you sort me out for lunch" becomes "this week's total is $14 for you", and the chasing tone drops out. If the phrasing itself is the sticking point, the examples in [how to ask a friend to pay you back](/en/blog/asking-friends-to-pay-back/) transfer to the office almost unchanged.

## 🏢 Colleagues are a relationship you can't walk away from

With friends, writing off a small amount is a real option. A few dollars is a rounding error against everything else in the friendship.

Work is different. **You see these people daily, you don't choose the seating, and it runs until one of you leaves.** Under those conditions the person who let it go keeps a small grievance, and it surfaces somewhere else — turning down the invitation, never getting a say in where you eat. That's a far worse outcome than talking about money.

Recording and closing isn't about distrusting anyone. It's the arrangement that suits a relationship expected to last: nothing gets carried forward.

## ❓ Common questions

**Q. My manager often insists on paying. Should I still record it?**
Log it, but don't settle it. Having the fact written down makes it easier to judge when it's your turn to offer. Trying to pay them back turns down what they meant by it.

**Q. The group changes size day to day.**
Pick the participants on each expense as you go. Totals stay correct however much the headcount moves, as long as you split per expense. Managing a per-head figure in your head stops working the first time someone drops out.

**Q. What if they transfer departments before I've asked?**
Close it out once the move is confirmed. A send-off is a moment when money talk costs nothing socially. Miss it and you lose the everyday contact that makes collecting easy.

**Q. Some of us pay cash, some by card.**
The payment method doesn't affect the record. Keep track of who paid what and the money can come back any way at all. Mixing methods isn't the problem, so only the record format needs to be consistent.
