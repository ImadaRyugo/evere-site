Funeral and memorial accounts have two features ordinary shared spending doesn't. One person almost always fronts the entire amount, and money also arrives, from guests.

Put those together and you get **an account where even the person running it can't say what they personally absorbed**, because money has been flowing both ways through the same pocket.

## 🕯️ The person who fronts it never sends the bill

Venues, celebrants, flowers and caterers all get paid on the day or the day before, by whoever is organising. There's no window in which to collect money in advance.

The trouble comes afterwards. By the time everyone has gone back to their lives, the moment to raise it has quietly closed. **The larger the amount, the harder it becomes to mention** — the exact opposite of how it ought to work — and one person ends up absorbing all of it.

What needs deciding in advance isn't the amount, it's the date. One sentence on the day, "I'll circulate the figures afterwards and we'll divide it then," makes everything that follows possible.

## 🧾 Sort the costs into three kinds

Trying to split one lump sum stalls immediately, because different kinds of cost are tangled inside it.

| Category | Examples | Who carries it |
|---|---|---|
| The ceremony | Venue, officiant, flowers, order of service | Split between relatives |
| The gathering | Catering, drinks, the meal afterwards | Split between relatives (varies with numbers) |
| Personal costs | Travel to attend, hotel, clothing | Each person |

Leaving travel with the individual can look harsh on relatives who live far away. Fold it into the shared pot, though, and the difference in distance turns into a permanent unfairness. It is the same structure as [the cost of visiting family](/en/blog/visiting-parents-costs/): nobody chose where they live, which is why the treatment has to be agreed rather than assumed.

## 💐 Money from guests is income, so subtract it first

This is where the arithmetic genuinely differs. Guests bring contributions, so splitting the outgoings alone collects too much.

The order is: total spend, minus what came in, divide the shortfall.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Ceremony and catering | $4,800 |
| Thank-you gifts for guests | $600 |
| Contributions received | $1,800 |
| Shortfall | $3,600 |
| Split three ways | $1,200 |

The organiser paid out $5,400 and received $1,800, leaving them $3,600 out of pocket. Divided three ways that's $1,200 each, so the other two send $1,200 apiece and the organiser lands on $1,200 as well. Check it: $5,400 − $1,800 − $2,400 = $1,200.

None of this works unless someone wrote down what came in. Name a person to record each contribution as it arrives.

## 🧮 An equal split isn't the only defensible one

Three equal shares is the simplest arrangement and sometimes the wrong one.

Closer relatives take more. The generation still earning takes more. Whoever organised it takes less because they did the work. All of these hold up. **What matters is less which rule you pick than whether everyone heard the same rule.**

What to avoid is settling it verbally in a corridor with nobody writing it down. When the family gathers again for the next anniversary, no one will be able to reconstruct what was agreed.

## 📄 One sheet, the same sheet, to everybody

The report needs no craft. Date, what it was for, amount, total contributions, shortfall, share per person. That is the whole document.

The important part is that everyone reads the same copy. Explain it individually and each person walks away with a slightly different version. It is the principle from [keeping the books for a club trip](/en/blog/club-camp-accounting/) — the scale changes, the procedure doesn't.

[Create a project](/en/docs/projects/) in Evere, enter the outgoings, and the totals and per-person shares come out on the screen. For the contributions, entering only the net shortfall is easier to read than recording each gift as a reverse payment.

## ❓ Common questions

**Q. If it comes out of the parent's own savings, is there anything to split?**
Sometimes not. Even then, tell everyone who holds that account and how much left it. Without a record, nobody can explain it when inheritance comes up later.

**Q. What if contributions exceed the costs?**
Don't let the surplus become anyone's income. Decide whether it's held for the next anniversary or divided on the spot, and if it's held, write down who is holding how much.

**Q. Do we bill relatives who couldn't attend?**
Attending and contributing are separate things, so ask them first. Leaving out someone who intended to contribute causes more offence than asking.

**Q. Someone doesn't want the amounts shown to the wider family.**
You can't withhold the total from people you're asking to pay; the basis has to be visible. If individual line items are the concern, a summary by category is enough.
