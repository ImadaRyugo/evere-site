Even couples who split everything smoothly the rest of the year need a different rule for birthdays and anniversaries. **Bring up splitting on those days and a question about money turns into a question about feelings.**

Making everything a treat doesn't solve it either, because then the balance between what each of you spends starts to nag. The way out of both is to treat the days differently from each other.

## 🎂 Birthdays and anniversaries aren't the same thing

A birthday has one lead. The person being celebrated is a guest, so they don't pay. Splitting the bill on that day cuts the gesture in half.

An anniversary, or Christmas, is a day the two of you arrive at from the same position. There's no lead, so there's no reason for one person to carry all of it. Halving it or alternating are both natural.

That distinction alone settles most of the decisions. **Days with a lead are treats, days without one get split.** When you're unsure, ask whose day it is and the answer follows.

## 🎁 Count the gift and the dinner as one budget

Birthday spending usually splits into the present and the meal. Judge them separately and you invite the comparison where somebody looks at one half and concludes this year was thinner.

Add them together. Spending less on the gift and more on the restaurant, or the reverse, is just allocation inside one budget. What actually delights someone changes year to year, so having the freedom to shift the balance produces better birthdays.

If the person being celebrated insists on paying for dinner, don't fight it. When the guest of honour offers, it's usually consideration rather than a statement about money. Accept it on the day and return it at the next opportunity.

## 🧮 A year side by side shows the real balance

Treating birthdays as gifts produces a gap. Whether that gap should bother you is much easier to judge once a year of it is laid out.

| Occasion | Who paid | Amount | A's share | B's share |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A's birthday | B | $180 | $0 | $180 |
| B's birthday | A | $120 | $120 | $0 |
| Anniversary dinner | Split | $200 | $100 | $100 |

A's year comes to 120 + 100 = $220 and B's to 180 + 100 = $280. The total is $500 and the gap is $60.

The important part is not closing that $60. What a birthday costs is set by the person giving, so equalising it stops it being a gift. **Settle only the days you agreed to split**, and record the treat days without asking them to balance. That line is what keeps a couple's accounting light.

## 💬 Say "smaller this year" in advance

There will be a year when you want to spend less. Just after a job change, a move, a large purchase. Quietly halving the budget in that year reads to the other person as cooling off.

Say it beforehand, not on the day, and give the reason once and briefly. "We've just moved, so can we do dinner only this year?" is enough. A long explanation sounds like an excuse, so state the fact and stop.

Saying it early also lets them adjust their own plans. The situation where one person turns up with something lavish and the other doesn't is entirely preventable with one sentence. Raising money early was the point of [splitting the bill on a first date](/en/blog/first-date-bill-split/) too, and the value of doing it only grows as the relationship lengthens.

## 🔁 If you alternate, keep a record of whose turn it is

Alternating on anniversaries feels effortless, but an event that happens a few times a year is guaranteed to be forgotten. When neither of you remembers who paid last time, whoever reaches first pays again, and that becomes the pattern.

You don't need detailed accounting for this. The bare fact of who paid is enough. [Logging a single expense](/en/docs/expenses/) in Evere takes seconds, and checking it before the next occasion tells you whose turn it is.

## ❓ Common questions

**Q. We earn different amounts. Should birthday budgets differ too?**
They can. What a gift costs is set by its weight inside your own finances, not by the size of your feelings. Stretching to match somebody else's budget produces a strain that always resurfaces somewhere else.

**Q. They gave me something expensive. Do I match it?**
No. Matching the amount turns a gift into an exchange. If you feel the imbalance, return it in effort or in occasions rather than in money.

**Q. Do we have to mark every anniversary every year?**
Reducing the number of occasions is completely fine as long as you both want to. An occasion that has become an obligation keeps its price tag and loses its content. When you cut back, choose together which days stay.

**Q. What if a trip replaces the anniversary dinner?**
Split it or use a ratio rather than treating it. The amounts are large and they keep growing while you're away, so having one person carry all of it also puts every on-the-spot decision on that person. Agreeing the split beforehand means you never discuss money while you're there.
