Choosing a group gift is the enjoyable part. It gets difficult after the purchase, when the same amount has to come back from every single person.

One person never replies. Naming the figure feels grabby. The total doesn't divide cleanly. And it ends with whoever suggested the gift quietly covering the gap. The present goes down well, and the organiser is the only one who takes something unpleasant home. This piece is about removing that last part.

## 🎁 Decide the cap before you decide the gift

The most common mistake is finding something lovely and then telling people what it costs. To someone already sold on the idea that reads as a discussion. To someone who hasn't been involved yet, it reads as an invoice.

Reverse the order. Before anyone starts shopping, send the range: "around $30 each, no more than $35." That single line lets people decide by the number rather than by the object, which is what makes opting out possible without embarrassment.

Set the cap for the person with the least room, not the average. In a group that mixes students and full-time salaries, pricing it for the salaries produces **people who quietly decline without saying why**. Fewer contributors then raises the per-head figure, which pushes out the next person, and the whole thing unravels in that order.

## 📣 How you ask changes how much comes back

Collection goes far more smoothly when you separate the invitation from the request. The invitation goes to everyone; the request goes to one person at a time.

The invitation needs only four facts: what it is, when it's being handed over, the amount each, and the deadline. Naming the handover date first gives the deadline a reason, so tie it to the event: "we're giving it at Friday's dinner, so anything by Thursday works."

Then keep your own list of who has paid. Posting "still waiting on a few of you" into the group chat repeatedly is the least efficient chase there is, because everyone who already paid gets the same notification.

## 🧮 The remainder on $230 across seven people

Split $230 seven ways and you get $32.857 each, which is not a number you can ask anyone for. That leaves three ways to handle it.

| Method | Each pays | Total collected | What happens to the difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Round up, everyone equal | $33 | $231 | The $1 goes towards the card |
| Organiser takes the remainder | $33 (other six) | $230 | Organiser pays $32 |
| Set the cap first | $35 | $245 | $15 buys flowers as well |

Checking the arithmetic: rounding up is 7 × $33 = $231, the organiser-absorbs row is 6 × $33 + $32 = $230, and the cap row is 7 × $35 = $245.

In practice the third row causes the fewest arguments. Set the contribution so the collected amount lands above the price of the gift, then spend the surplus on the card, the wrapping, or flowers on the day. **A collection with no cash to hand back finishes in a single round**, and handing change back is what turns a two-day job into a two-week one.

## 💸 Keeping the buyer out of pocket

Somebody has to put the whole amount on one card. The only way to make that easier is to change the order of events.

Collect first, buy second, and nobody fronts anything. Use that order whenever the total is large. If stock or a same-day discount forces you to buy first, then fix the amount and send it to everyone the moment you leave the shop. Every day you wait makes the money harder to get back.

Splitting the roles helps too. The buyer only has to report the receipt total; the collector only has to collect. Above about ten contributors, doing both jobs alone means spending the whole week on messages.

## 🃏 When someone wants in at the last minute

Being asked to be included the night before is normal. There's no need to say no, but do not recalculate the shares.

Lowering what everyone already paid means issuing refunds, and that work costs more than the extra contribution is worth. Ask the latecomer for the same amount and put the surplus towards the card or one more small item, or let them take the organiser's job next time. Being able to say **the figure doesn't move once it's announced** turns this into a rule you can reuse at the next occasion.

Keeping a record makes that call quicker. [Log the purchase once and pick who shares it](/en/docs/expenses/) in Evere and who paid what stays visible afterwards. ## ❓ Common questions

**Q. What if one person simply never pays?**
Hand the gift over as planned. Collecting after the handover damages far less than arguing before it. Keep the amount recorded, and raise it once, in person, the next time you see them. A record that exists works better than repeated reminders.

**Q. The recipient wants to know what it cost. Do I tell them?**
Give the number of contributors, not the total. Once someone knows the figure, they start planning a gift of matching size in return.

**Q. Cash and app transfers together are hard to track.**
Accept one method. Allowing two means checking two places for incoming money, which is exactly how an unpaid share gets missed. If a few people can only do cash, identify them at the start and treat them as the named exception.

**Q. Can the organiser contribute less?**
Taking the shopping and the handover off everyone else makes a small discount, roughly the size of the remainder, reasonable. Just don't reduce it silently. If you're taking less, put it in the first message.
