A $50-a-head dinner with a drinks package, and the person who had three sodas pays $50. Plenty of people find that hard to accept, and almost none of them say so at the table.

It's hard to raise because **a question about money turns instantly into a question about character**. Nobody wants to be the person who soured the evening over ten dollars, so paying quietly is the cheaper option. That calculation is what produces the even split, every time.

## 🥤 The resentment isn't really about the amount

What bothers a non-drinker usually isn't the gap itself. It's ten or twelve dollars a time, and they'd be the first to say that's survivable.

The problem is frequency. Two dinners a month for a year is twenty-four of those gaps. **An amount that's genuinely fine once stops being fine when it repeats.** That structure isn't specific to drinking; it's behind almost every splitting grievance there is.

The second half of it is the sense that nobody has noticed. When the rest of the table is unaware that someone is subsidising them, the subsidy doesn't even earn thanks. You can leave the money exactly as it is and still remove most of the resentment, simply by having the fact acknowledged.

## 💸 Work out the real gap first

Before anyone argues, calculate the difference. Most venues publish both prices, so the drinks element is usually sitting right there on the menu.

If the food-only set is $35 and the version with the drinks package is $50, the drinks are $15. Once that number exists, the rest is just allocation. Everyone looking at the figure 15 shortens the conversation considerably compared with trading impressions about who feels overcharged.

For à la carte evenings you can't do this cleanly, so total the drinks lines on the bill instead. Being in the right ballpark matters more than being exact.

## ⚖️ Three ways to tilt it

The first is a straight even split. It needs no arithmetic and gets you out of the restaurant fastest. If only one or two people aren't drinking, plenty of groups choose this, and it isn't wrong.

The second removes the drinks element completely, so non-drinkers pay only for food. It's the cleanest logic, but the drinkers' share visibly rises, which can meet resistance when they're the majority.

The third splits the difference in half. It rests on the idea that everyone at the table is also buying the evening itself, not just what's in their glass. The reasoning is admittedly fuzzy, but the fuzziness is exactly what takes the edge off, which is why it tends to work best in practice.

## 🗣️ The real problem is who raises it

Harder than choosing an option is deciding who says it out loud. When the non-drinker raises it, they're arguing to reduce their own bill, which is the most uncomfortable version of the conversation.

This is a job for whoever is organising. Put "non-drinkers pay $35" in the invitation and nobody has to negotiate anything. **A rule that exists before the event belongs to nobody in particular**, which is the whole point.

Designated drivers, pregnancy, people who simply can't process alcohol, people who quit. You don't need to know which. Make it self-declared and don't ask why, and the friction disappears along with the explanations.

## 🧮 Eight people, two not drinking

A $400 total across eight people, where the drinks package accounts for $15 a head and two people aren't drinking.

| Approach | The 2 not drinking | The 6 drinking | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Even split | $50 | $50 | $400 |
| Drinks removed | $35 | $55 | $400 |
| Half the difference | $42.50 | $52.50 | $400 |

Each row totals $400: 2 × 35 + 6 × 55, or 2 × 42.50 + 6 × 52.50.

What's worth noticing is how little the drinkers' side moves. Against an even $50, removing the drinks entirely costs them $5 more, and the halfway version costs $2.50. **The unfairness the non-drinker feels is large; the adjustment the drinkers pay for it is small.** That asymmetry is the whole argument.

## ❓ Common questions

**What about someone who has one drink?**
Let them self-declare. Create a category for the one-glass person and you'll immediately need one for two glasses, and it never ends. Two categories, chosen by the individual, is the version that survives contact with reality.

**Someone arrived an hour late.**
Drinks packages are priced by time, so reducing a late arrival's share is defensible. The venue's bill doesn't shrink, though, so the difference lands on everyone else. Drawing a line at, say, an hour keeps this from being renegotiated every time.

**Doesn't a tilt just make everyone self-conscious?**
Only once, if it's decided in advance and published. Deciding it fresh at every dinner produces that awkwardness once per dinner instead. Making it a fixed rule is the least self-conscious option available.

**The drinkers say not to bother. Should we drop it?**
If you take that at face value and revert to an even split, the non-drinker is back to being unable to raise it. Even when you decide not to adjust the money, make sure everyone knows what the gap is. A burden that's been acknowledged is a different thing from one that hasn't.
