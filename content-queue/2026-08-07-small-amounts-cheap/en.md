You bought a friend a coffee at the corner shop. $4.20.

Whether you can say "get me back for that" is something people genuinely agonise over. Ask and you might look petty. Don't ask and you're out the money. Neither outcome amounts to much, and yet the decision takes real time.

That small amounts are harder to raise than large ones is, when you sit with it, a strange fact.

## Why small amounts are the hard ones

Front someone $300 and nobody hesitates. They expect to pay it back and you expect to say something. When the number is large, asking is administrative.

$4.20 isn't administrative. Saying $4.20 out loud carries meaning beyond the money. It tells the other person that you are someone who tracks amounts at that level. What you're asking about is a coffee; what gets communicated is a description of your character.

So people stay quiet. Quiet, and remembering slightly.

## The amounts you forget and the ones you don't

The trouble starts when you don't forget.

A one-off $4.20 genuinely does vanish from memory for most people, and nothing residual attaches to the friendship. The problem case is when it repeats.

The same person always gets there first at the weekly lunch. Even at $12 a time, that's $48 a month, and $288 over six months. At that point it isn't a small amount anymore. And yet, precisely because you didn't say anything the first time, saying something now is harder than it would have been at the start.

Asking about small amounts isn't awkward because any single instance is awkward. It's awkward because **the instances you let pass accumulate until there's no natural moment left to raise it**.

## Who gets called petty and who doesn't

Two people can ask about the same small amount and only one of them gets described as cheap.

The difference isn't the amount. It's **consistency and direction**.

Someone who asks when they've fronted money but stays quiet when the other person has gets described that way regardless of the sums involved. Meanwhile, someone who volunteers what they owe as readily as what they're owed can ask about $8 and it lands fine. What comes across is that they're talking about a record rather than a balance in their favour.

Timing does work too. "That's $4.20, then" said at the counter reads completely differently from a message three days later. The second one tells the other person you've been carrying it around.

Which is to say: what determines whether you look petty is your own posture, not the number.

## Build a system that doesn't accumulate and you never have to ask

The best outcome is one where asking never happens.

The method is straightforward: **log it at the time and net it off later**. You don't need the $4.20 now. It just needs to come out in the wash the next time they cover something.

Once that's running, the occasion for saying a small number out loud disappears. "Get me back" becomes "let's settle up for the month", and no character description rides along with it. It's just a list of figures.

For a group of friends, monthly works, or after a trip. Fixing a boundary means nothing has to be said until that day arrives. Not saying anything stops being restraint and becomes ordinary waiting.

Wherever you keep the record, more than one person has to be able to see it. A number in your own notes is, from the other side, a number you are claiming. A shared record is simply a fact. The gap between those two is wide.

## Deciding not to ask is also an answer

Having written all of the above: not everything needs to be recorded.

What you've decided to treat as your treat is healthier left off the list. The birthday round, the time you covered someone who was struggling, the restaurant you chose and dragged everyone to. Put those in a record and a sense that they were loans grows around them later.

What matters is that **you decide, rather than drift**. Let it slide with a vague "never mind" and it stays with you unforgotten. The moment you decide it was a gift, that $4.20 is finished as far as you're concerned.

Where you draw the line is yours. It's only the absence of a line that comes back later.
