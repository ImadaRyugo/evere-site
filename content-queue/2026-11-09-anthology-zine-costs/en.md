Start a group zine and the deadlines and cover design come together fast. Money gets pushed to later. Then, on the day files go to the printer, the organizer pays the whole bill alone.

From there, **the person who paid the printer, the person holding the sales money and the person taking home the leftover boxes tend to be the same person.** The convention ends with one person carrying all of it.

## 📚 Zine money gathers around whoever pays the printer

For a group zine, the organizer usually submits the files and pays the printer on everyone's behalf. One order means one payment, so the money naturally runs through the organizer.

Printing is paid around the time files go in, which is long before any copies sell, whether through pre-orders or at a table. Table fees and shipping work the same way. In practice, the organizer is fronting everyone's costs for weeks or months.

Who carries how much of that advance? To answer that, you first have to separate the two kinds of contributors.

## 🧮 Separate the people who chip in from the people who only contribute work

A zine's contributors play one of two roles. Some chip in on costs and become co-owners of the book. Others are guest contributors who only send work.

Those who chip in share the print bill, and in exchange get a share of the sales and the leftover stock. Guests pay nothing, receive no sales, and typically get a contributor copy. Both are part of the zine, but on the money side they are treated differently.

**Tell people which role they are in when you invite them.** Hear "can we split the print bill?" after files go in, and someone who thought they were a guest will feel it was sprung on them.

## 💴 Sales go first toward the print-cost gap

Here is an example. Six contributors: A, B, C and D chip in, and the other two are guests. A, the organizer, prints 100 copies for $1,200. Setting aside six contributor copies, 94 copies sell at $15 each.

Seventy-two copies sell at the convention, and A, who ran the table, collects the $1,080.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Printing (A paid the printer) | $1,200 |
| Convention sales (72 × $15, collected by A) | $1,080 |
| Shortfall | $120 |
| Split among the four who chip in | $30 each |

A paid the printer and A holds the sales, so only the difference needs to move. B, C and D each send A $30, and that settles things for now. **There is no need to collect $300 from everyone up front and then hand sales back out.**

Group zines are often priced at or below the cost of printing. If a loss is expected, say so to contributors in advance, so nobody is surprised by the shortfall at the end of the day.

## 📦 Who owns the leftover copies?

In the example, 22 copies are left. Leave their fate undecided and it becomes a source of friction.

One option is for the organizer to keep the stock and, as copies sell at later conventions or online, pass the money to the four who chipped in until their $30 each is recovered. If all 22 sell, that is $330: everyone's $30 comes back and there is a surplus, split among the same four.

The other is to divide the stock among the four and let each sell or keep their share. That cuts down on tracking, but the organizer can no longer see what sold.

Either works. **What matters is deciding before the convention who takes the stock home and where the money from it goes.** The thinking behind selling something owned in common is also covered in [you sold the thing you bought together](/en/blog/selling-shared-items/).

## ❓ Common questions

**Q. Do table fees and shipping get split along with printing?**
Yes. Both are costs of putting out the zine, so split them among the people who chip in, same as printing. In Evere, record printing, table fees and shipping as separate payments, and the [settle-up screen](/en/docs/settlement/) gives you the total.

**Q. Contributors' page counts vary a lot.**
You can split costs by page count. If you do, split sales and stock by the same ratio so it stays consistent. In Evere, [Ratio](/en/docs/expenses/) lets you enter page shares directly.

**Q. The organizer offered to cover everything. Do we still need records?**
It is still worth recording the print cost and sales. Next time, how many you printed and how many were left is the best guide to your print run and price.

**Q. We want to donate any profit to charity.**
Plenty of zines do. Tell every contributor when you invite them. Whether those who chipped in recover their costs first, or every dollar of sales is donated, changes what each person ends up paying.
