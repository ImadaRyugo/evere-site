The projector three of you bought, the washing machine from the house share, the big tent the club paid for. Anything owned jointly eventually gets sold.

And that is the moment everyone remembers that **the original contributions weren't equal.** Without a record of who put in what, the basis for dividing the money becomes whatever people recall.

## 💸 You're dividing a return, not a profit

Get the language right first. Money from selling something you owned together is not profit.

If $1,200 went in and $480 comes out, the $720 difference is value consumed while you used it. The $480 is what's left of the original asset, converted back to cash.

That framing matters because **treating it as profit makes an even split feel natural.** Treating it as a returning asset turns it into a question of whose asset is coming back, and the answer follows automatically.

## 🧮 Reuse the ratio of what people put in

That's the whole rule: divide the sale by the contribution ratio.

Three people bought a $1,200 projector with $600, $400 and $200. The ratio is 3:2:1. Two years later it sells for $480.

| | Put in | Ratio | Share of sale |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | $600 | 3 | $240 |
| B | $400 | 2 | $160 |
| C | $200 | 1 | $80 |
| Total | $1,200 | 6 | $480 |

Split evenly at $160 each and C, who contributed $200, gets $160 back — almost everything. A, who put in $600, drops from $240 to $160. **The more someone contributed, the worse an even split treats them.**

By ratio, everyone recovers the same 40%. More in, more back; less in, less back. Needing no explanation is the method's main advantage.

Reconstructing the ratio years later is hard, so record it at the time of purchase. ## 🛠️ Take the seller's work off the top

Selling is work. Photographs, a description, pricing, answering questions, packing, posting. For anything large, meeting the buyer as well.

Let one person do all that unpaid and nobody volunteers next time. Charging an hourly rate isn't realistic either.

The practical version is to deduct a set amount from the sale before dividing it. Sold for $480, take $30 as a handling fee and divide the remaining $450 by the ratio. **The size of the figure matters less than the fact that the work has a price at all.** Postage and packaging come off the top the same way.

## 📉 Nobody is to blame for depreciation

When something sells for less than expected, people start looking for a reason. We should have sold it sooner; he scratched it; it was stored badly.

Let that go. **Agreeing to own something jointly means agreeing to lose value on it jointly.** Short of clear negligence, falling value is simply what ownership does.

The exception is visible damage. Even then, handle it the way you'd handle a repair bill: the responsible person covers only the difference between what it fetched and what it would have fetched repaired. Make them carry the whole thing and nobody will ever use it again.

## 📜 Decide how you'll decide to sell

The most overlooked part: joint ownership needs a procedure for deciding to sell at all.

One of three wants to sell and two want to keep it. Majority, unanimity, or does the objector buy out the other two shares?

Undecided, the thing hangs in limbo for years. Nobody uses it, nobody lets it go, and whoever is storing it keeps carrying that. Dividing the contents of a home works the same way: as [settling up when you stop living together](/en/blog/cohabitation-breakup-settlement/) puts it, what needs deciding isn't the possessions but the procedure.

One sentence at the time of purchase is enough. A majority can sell; anyone who objects may buy the other shares at the residual value on that date. Then the day it goes, nothing stalls.
