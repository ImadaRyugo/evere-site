Finish a meal around a round table with friends from China or Taiwan and a small tussle may break out at the end. "I've got this." "No, I've got it." Before you know it, someone has already paid at the register. If you're used to splitting by default, you may never find the right moment to suggest it.

In the Chinese-speaking world, **請客 (qǐngkè), where one person pays for everyone, and AA, the word for splitting the bill, are chosen depending on the situation**. Neither is the right answer; what helps is knowing which one a given situation calls for.

## Qingke: the person who invites pays

Traditionally at a Chinese-speaking meal, the person who extended the invitation, or whoever is playing host, pays for everyone. At a birthday or a promotion, the person being celebrated treats; at a business meal, the inviting side pays. It's also common for the oldest or most senior person to pay.

The tug-of-war over the bill is the flip side of this custom. **Offering to pay is seen as a sign that you value the other person**, and backing down too quickly can actually come across as indifferent. You don't need to fight in earnest, though. Offer a couple of times, and if they still insist, thank them and accept.

After being treated, say 下次我请 (xià cì wǒ qǐng), "next time is on me." A treat in the Chinese-speaking world isn't a one-off gift; it carries on as **an understanding about whose turn it is next**.

## AA: splitting is standard among friends

Among friends of the same age, especially younger people, AA has become the norm. A quick 我们AA吧 (wǒmen AA ba, "let's split it") and the bill is divided evenly. A 2010 language article in China Daily described AA as the arrangement for most get-togethers, apart from company year-end banquets and occasions where someone is treating ([China Daily](https://language.chinadaily.com.cn/trans/2010-02/09/content_9451889.htm)).

When each person pays only for what they ordered, the phrase is 各付各的 (gè fù gè de).

## Split features on the phone

On the mainland, most bill-splitting happens inside a phone. WeChat's 群收款 (group collection) lets you enter an amount in a group chat and request payment from everyone, with notification reminders for anyone who hasn't paid. Alipay has offered a similar feature, AA收款, which takes a total and a headcount, works out the per-person amount, and collects it.

In Taiwan, LINE Pay Money, LINE Pay's e-wallet account service, has a 分帳 (split the bill) feature that can divide a total evenly or set a different amount for each person. Transfer apps such as JKOPAY are also widely used.

Most of these assume a mainland or Taiwanese phone number or bank account, though, so visitors usually can't use them as is.

## How Taiwan differs from the mainland

Taiwan uses qingke and AA in a similar way, with some differences in wording and tone. AA is understood, but you'll also hear 各付各的 and 平分 (píngfēn, split evenly) often. Among students and friends of the same age, paying your own is an easy default, while a gathering with elders or a celebration tends to mean someone treats. That much is the same as on the mainland.

In both places, if the other person is older, accepting and returning the favor next time goes down better than insisting on splitting. On the other hand, letting a friend your own age pay every time puts a burden on them, so suggest AA or take the next bill yourself.

## Tips for eating out with Chinese-speaking friends

If you come from a place where splitting is the unspoken default, it helps to set that assumption aside when eating with Chinese-speaking friends.

Start by noticing who invited whom. **If you did the inviting, offering to pay is the courteous move.** If they invited you, offer once, and if they decline, accept and return the favor next time. For a casual meal among friends, saying "let's do AA" up front means there's no tussle when the bill comes.

When treats go back and forth for a while, it gets hard to tell who has paid how much. There's no need to keep exact score, but so it doesn't tilt too far one way, **just remember who paid how many times**, and you can take your turn naturally.
