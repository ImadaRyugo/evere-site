Freelancers who work together accumulate shared tools without ever deciding to. A design app, file storage, an invoicing service.

The trouble doesn't start while you're using them. It starts **when somebody leaves**. That's the moment everyone finds out whose name the account is in, who holds the controls, and whose card the money comes off.

## One name on the account means one person holds the controls

"Shared subscription" almost always means one person subscribed and everyone else chips in. Only one card can be on file.

Which puts three roles on the same person: the payer, the administrator, and the only one who can cancel. The cost is split, but **the power to switch it off sits with the account holder alone**.

That's where it goes wrong. If the account holder winds down their workload and decides they don't need it, everyone else's working setup disappears. In the other direction, the account holder can stop using it entirely and still watch the charge come off their card every month. Agreeing how to split the cost without ever discussing control is the most common version of this mistake.

## The annual discount breaks monthly splitting

Most tools discount an annual plan. If $60 a month becomes $600 a year, that's $120 saved, so of course you take it.

But paying annually makes the maths much harder the moment the headcount changes.

| Billing | Three people for a year | One leaves after six months |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly | $20 each per month | Split two ways from that month |
| Annual | $200 each per year | No refund, so it needs apportioning |

Annual billing locks the spend in advance, so you have to decide whether someone absorbs the leaver's share or whether unused months get paid back pro rata. **Choose annual and you're choosing to settle the mid-term exit calculation at the same time.** Pick it purely on price and that calculation lands on you later.

## The receipt only goes to the account holder

For a freelancer, tool costs are business expenses, which means you need a receipt.

On a shared subscription, the service issues exactly one receipt, addressed to the account holder. Everyone else has no document proving the share they paid.

There are two routes: the account holder invoices the others, or the account holder expenses the full amount and treats incoming payments as recharged costs rather than revenue. Which one you use has tax consequences, so agree on a single treatment before you start. One person handling it differently guarantees a mismatch at filing time.

## Agree the exit terms before you sign up

Shared subscriptions end for ordinary reasons. Someone takes a staff job, stops using the tool, or can no longer justify the rate.

Three things to settle:

- How much notice someone gives (a month before renewal, on an annual plan)
- Whether unused months on an annual plan are refunded
- What happens to the leaver's files and data

The third gets forgotten. Files in shared storage can disappear along with the account. For the person leaving, that's their work history rather than their money, which can matter a great deal more than the refund.

## Who decides when the price goes up

Subscriptions raise their prices. On a shared account, that notice only reaches one person.

If the account holder quietly absorbs it, everyone else's contribution rises without their knowing. If the account holder switches tools unilaterally, everyone else's work stops. **One line agreed at the start — price change notices get forwarded — prevents both.**

Setting the threshold early saves time too. A rule that anything above a certain increase goes back to the group means you aren't opening a debate every renewal. ## Common questions

**Q. Shouldn't we just put it in a company name?**
Forming a company purely to hold tool subscriptions is far too heavy. Writing the contract and control rules down and sharing them is the practical version.

**Q. We'd rather split by usage than by head.**
Per-seat tools divide by headcount cleanly. For usage-based pricing, check first whether the billing breaks down per person. If it doesn't, per head is the only option.

**Q. Someone keeps paying late.**
The structure has the account holder fronting the money, so ignoring it leaves one person permanently lending to the others. Set a closing date and bring everything level there. The approach in [a monthly settlement day stops the arguments](/en/blog/monthly-settlement-day/) applies directly.

**Q. What if someone doesn't use it for a month?**
Adding and removing people monthly is more admin than it's worth. If the unused stretches are long when you look at a year, that person is better off out of the arrangement entirely.
