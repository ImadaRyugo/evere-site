Handed the money for a club or society trip, most people assume the first task is collecting it. It isn't. **The first task is deciding the shape you'll be able to explain afterwards.**

What makes trip accounting hard isn't the size of the amounts or the number of people. It's that cash mixes with transfers, prepayments mix with things bought on the day, and several different people end up paying for things. Run all three at once without a fixed way of recording, and nothing is reconstructable by the end.

## 📒 The job is being able to explain it

After the trip, a treasurer gets asked two questions. What did it cost in the end, and what happened to the money left over.

Answer both immediately and you've done the job. Collect flawlessly but fail to answer them, and a residue of suspicion remains. **Being able to trace the path counts for more than precision to the penny**, which is the part of the role people underestimate.

So the first decision is granularity. Every receipt, or just a total per category? For a trip of twenty-four people, per category is plenty: accommodation, transport, food, equipment, miscellaneous. If something turns up that doesn't fit those five, add a line then.

## 🧾 Put money in and money out in one ledger

Accounting falls apart when what you collected and what you spent live in two separate places. Reconciling two tables becomes its own task, and that task always gets postponed.

Use one ledger, in date order.

| Date | Item | In | Out | Balance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Aug | Trip fee, 24 × $80 | $1,920 | | $1,920 |
| 5 Aug | Accommodation (prepaid) | | $1,500 | $420 |
| 20 Aug | Coach | | $360 | $60 |
| 21 Aug | Extra food shop | | $45 | $15 |

1,920 minus 1,500 leaves $420, minus 360 leaves $60, and minus 45 leaves $15. Kept this way, **the balance is a single number you can state at any moment**.

Anything somebody fronted goes in the same ledger. "20 Aug, coach, paid by Nadia" on the out line is enough. Who gets repaid is a separate job you do once, after you're home.

## 🏦 Keep subs and trip fees apart

If your society holds annual subscriptions, don't put trip money in the same pot. Merge them and the trip's result disappears inside the subscription balance.

There's a second reason. Subs are money that passes to next year's committee; trip fees are money spent entirely on this trip. **Running two different kinds of money through one balance guarantees a discrepancy at handover.** If you can't have separate accounts, at least keep separate ledgers.

When the society subsidises part of the trip, record the subsidy as a single line under In. Put $300 from society funds at the top of the ledger and the amount the members paid stays visibly distinct from the amount the society paid.

## 🧮 Decide where leftovers go before you collect

In the example above, $15 was left. Across twenty-four people that's about sixty cents each, and refunding it costs far more effort than it's worth.

So put one line in the sign-up message. "Anything left over goes into society funds" works; so does "we'll spend it on the last night". As long as it's decided, nobody minds. Decided by the treasurer after the fact, it becomes a story about someone acting unilaterally, regardless of the sum.

Shortfalls need the same treatment. Top-up collection or society funds, agreed in advance, so the treasurer isn't the one absorbing it. **Once it becomes customary for the treasurer to eat the deficit, nobody takes the job next year.**

## 📤 Finish with one page

When you're back, don't publish the raw ledger. Write a summary instead: the total, the per-person figure, the main categories, and what happened to the remainder. Those four lines are enough.

You don't need to disclose individual receipts. Being able to produce them if asked is enough. Publishing everything up front invites commentary on each line and turns a closed matter into a week of messages.

Don't carry the whole thing alone either. Separating the person who books, the person who collects and the person who records makes each job small.
