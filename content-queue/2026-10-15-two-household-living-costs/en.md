A posting to another city, a weekend marriage, a life split between a weekday flat and a family home. The reasons vary and the effect on the budget doesn't.

**Two homes, one life.** That combination costs money in a way neither living together nor living apart does.

## 🏙️ What actually doubles

Separate what doubles from what doesn't.

Costs attached to a dwelling double: rent, standing charges, broadband, contents insurance, and the stock of everyday supplies. Toothpaste, detergent and cooking oil now exist in two places.

Costs attached to a person don't: insurance, phone, the total amount of food eaten. Nobody eats more because there are two kitchens.

Making that distinction first **separates the costs you can attack from the ones you can't.** The rent isn't going anywhere. The duplicated supplies come down as soon as one home is kept deliberately minimal.

## 🧮 Put a number on the duplication

Set one household against two.

| Item | One home | Two homes |
|---|---|---|
| Rent | $1,200 | $1,800 |
| Utilities | $180 | $230 |
| Broadband and phone lines | $80 | $130 |
| Total | $1,460 | $2,160 |

That's $700 a month, or $8,400 a year. Then add travelling to see each other: two round trips a month at $280 is $560. Together, $1,260 a month and $15,120 a year.

Producing that figure once is worth the effort, because whether to continue becomes a decision about a number rather than a feeling. For apportioning utilities across two properties, the method in [splitting utilities when one of you works from home](/en/blog/utilities-remote-work/) transfers directly.

## 🚄 Travel belongs to the household, not the traveller

Travel is where this usually goes wrong. Does the one who moved away come back, or does the one who stayed go out? Unequal trips, unequal burden.

Decide it simply. **Travel costs come out of the joint budget.** Who physically got on the train is irrelevant. The two of you chose this arrangement, so the travel it generates is a cost of the arrangement.

This differs from a long-distance relationship, where two separate budgets make the travelling person's costs pile up; that situation is covered in [the cost of a long-distance relationship](/en/blog/long-distance-relationship-costs/). Where the household is genuinely one, importing "whoever travels pays" only causes problems.

## 🏠 Naming a main home settles a lot of small questions

Treat both homes as equals and you generate endless small decisions. Which one gets the furniture, which one carries a big purchase, where the post goes.

Decide instead which one is the main home. That one is the centre of your life; the other is temporary.

The cost treatment then follows automatically. Investment in the main home is household spending; the temporary one stays minimal and gets treated as consumables. If the second place is a two-year posting, renting furniture beats buying it, and the reasoning arrives on its own. Sorting costs by their nature is the same move as in [setup costs when you move in together](/en/blog/moving-in-setup-costs/): **the question is how much you're willing to spend on money that doesn't come back.**

## 💼 Write down where the employer's contribution stops

Where a company covers part of the rent or some trips home, leaving the boundary vague makes the household record stop reconciling.

Two things to do. Confirm in writing what the employer covers. Then keep the employer's portion and the household's portion separate in your own records too.

If one trip home a month is covered, only the second trip is a household cost. Separating company expenses from personal ones works exactly as in [fronting the cost of a business trip](/en/blog/business-trip-advance/): split the single payment in two when you record it and month end takes care of itself.

You can also [keep two projects](/en/docs/projects/) in Evere — the main household, and the second location. Held apart, they leave you with an exact answer to what the arrangement cost once the posting ends.

## ❓ Common questions

**Q. Should our split ratio change when we go to two homes?**
The total rises, so an unchanged ratio raises both contributions. If incomes haven't moved, keep the ratio and treat absorbing the increase as a separate conversation.

**Q. One of us has stopped going back at all.**
If you're paying for a home nobody uses, it's time to check the arrangement against the numbers. Run the calculation above again.

**Q. Food costs more in two places.**
You lose the ability to buy in bulk, so the unit price rises. Budget for it rather than treating it as overspending, and the monthly estimate holds.

**Q. We're a weekend marriage with entirely separate finances.**
That works too. Manage only the shared spending jointly and leave housing to each person. Deciding not to mix them is the simplest approach available.
