Hobby gear is expensive. A full set runs into hundreds or thousands, and it gets used a handful of times a year.

Which is why somebody suggests buying one between two people. Half the cost, the same amount of use. It looks entirely rational, and for the first year it works.

## Borrowing and co-owning are not the same thing

Draw one distinction before anything else. Borrowing somebody's gear and jointly owning a thing look similar and behave completely differently.

When you borrow, ownership is unambiguous. Break it and you replace it, the owner maintains it, the owner decides when it goes out. No situation requires a judgement call.

Co-ownership removes that clarity. **Two owners means two people who get to decide.** You halve the cost and you also halve the speed of every decision.

## Who pays when it breaks sets the tone

The first fork in co-ownership is damage.

If someone was careless, that's a replacement conversation. What actually happens is vaguer: fabric perishes, a part wears out, a battery swells. Nobody's fault.

The principle here is simple — repairs divide by ownership share. Half each, so half the repair. What matters more is **agreeing in advance that damage occurring during use isn't automatically the user's fault.** Leave it ambiguous and people tense up every time they take it out. Tense people stop taking it out, and gear nobody takes out has no reason to be co-owned.

For club equipment there's the option of turning it into an annual cost over a fixed service life, as described in [buying team kit together](/en/blog/team-gear-group-buy/). Between two people you don't need machinery that formal. Saying out loud how repairs get handled is enough.

## Unequal use can't be fixed with money

A year in, the use is always uneven. One of you goes out monthly, the other twice a year.

At that point somebody suggests recalculating the original split by usage. Understandable, and it doesn't work. The purchase money is already spent, and the moment "I've barely used it, give some back" is said, the other person starts being careful about using it.

The realistic adjustment happens at the next outlay. When a repair, a replacement or consumables come round, the heavier user takes more of it. Don't re-cut the past; change the ratio going forward. That's the same move as separating fixed from variable costs in [upkeep on a family car or holiday home](/en/blog/family-shared-assets-upkeep/) — adjust only the part that moves and you get far less friction.

## Whoever stores it carries the most

There's a cost that rarely gets counted: keeping the thing.

Tents, bikes and boards take up room. It lives at one of your places, and that person loses a cupboard. Every outing, the other person either collects it or has it delivered. Maintenance and charging tend to fall on whoever's home it sits in.

**None of that gets invoiced, so none of it gets recorded.** It accumulates anyway. When "it is in my flat, so tell me before you take it" appears six months in, that is storage fatigue talking.

Offset it by having the other person cover consumables, or give the host a slightly larger share. The mechanism matters less than both of you knowing the cost exists.

## Agree the buyout price before you buy

Co-ownership gets worst when one person gives up the hobby.

Two people buy a $600 tent, use it three years, and one wants out. With no agreed basis, the conversation turns emotional. The one staying says it's still good, so half of $600 is $300. The one leaving says three years of use makes it $150. **Both positions are arguable.**

So agree the depreciation rule at purchase. Say the value reaches zero in five years: after three, two-fifths remain, which is $240, and each share is $120. The one staying pays $120 and the co-ownership ends.

The figure matters less than **the fact that the formula existed before the purchase did.** [Create a project](/en/docs/projects/) in Evere, record what it cost and what you agreed, and it's still there in three years. The odds of two people remembering a three-year-old conversation the same way are not good.
